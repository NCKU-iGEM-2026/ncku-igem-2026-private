Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are infections that patients acquire while they are being treated in a healthcare facility. They are common, difficult to treat, and costly.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is one of the bacteria that cause them, and one of the hardest to deal with. It resists many antibiotics, it releases toxins that damage host tissue, and it forms a biofilm that antibiotics struggle to cross. These behaviours depend on the cells acting together: they release chemical signals, detect them, and switch on the behaviours once enough signal has accumulated.

Capture is directed at those signals rather than at the bacteria. It consists of two systems that read the same molecule. The first detects the signal and reports the infection. The second captures the signal, so that the coordination it carries does not take place.

P. aeruginosa as a common cause

It is an opportunist, and it infects patients through the routes that medical care itself creates: ventilators, catheters and open wounds.

  • 2nd

    most common pathogen of healthcare-associated pneumonia in Taiwanese intensive care, and the most common in surgical-site infections in regional hospitals[1]

  • 20–40%

    mortality once a critically ill patient develops severe pneumonia or bacteraemia

  • 26.0%

    of P. aeruginosa isolates in Taiwanese medical-centre ICUs are carbapenem-resistant[1]

Mortality once an infection becomes severe is reported by Taiwan CDC; a study of medical-centre intensive care units in Taiwan puts mortality from P. aeruginosa pneumonia at 31.7%.

In the intensive care units of Taiwanese hospitals, the pathogens isolated most often from healthcare-associated pneumonia are Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, followed by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia in medical centres and Acinetobacter baumannii in regional hospitals. P. aeruginosa is second in both.[1] We chose to start with P. aeruginosa for two reasons. Its quorum sensing circuit is the best characterised of the three, and there is published work on its signal for both of the things we wanted to do: reading it, and binding it.[2][3][4][5] A. baumannii is our second candidate. A screen of 77 synthetic AHLs against its AbaR receptor identified 3-OH-C12-HSL as the most likely natural quorum sensing signal of that organism.[6]

Quorum sensing and the AHL signal

Bacteria coordinate their behaviour by releasing and detecting small signalling molecules, a process called quorum sensing. In P. aeruginosa these molecules are N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs). When enough cells are present, enough signal accumulates, and the population switches on the behaviours that are only useful in a group: swarming motility, which spreads the population across a surface; pyocyanin and other virulence factors, which damage host tissue; and biofilm formation.

The signal therefore has two properties we can use. It is present early, before the organism has been grown on a plate, and it is specific to the organism that produces it. Detecting the signal is therefore a way of detecting the organism, and removing the signal is a way of preventing the coordination it carries.

Limitations of current diagnosis

A ventilated patient is diagnosed by culturing sputum. The wait for that culture is where the problem starts.

{# The time problem and the antibiotic problem are one chain, so they are drawn as one: three plain line drawings with a few words each, rather than a paragraph the reader has to assemble the causality from. #}

  1. 48–72 hours

    Sputum is cultured to grow the organism. The result cannot show the state of the infection in real time, and antibiotics already given can produce a false negative. Susceptibility testing adds another 48–72 hours on top, so a full report often takes four to seven days.

  2. Treated on a guess

    The patient cannot wait, so treatment starts before the organism or its susceptibility is known. That means broad-spectrum cover, and often a last-line antibiotic held back for exactly this situation.

  3. Resistance

    Every empirical course selects for whatever survives it. The next infection starts from those survivors, and the drugs kept in reserve lose their value as the organism meets them more often.

Culture and susceptibility timings from the CIDRAP antimicrobial stewardship policy update and from a review of standard CLSI susceptibility testing.

Capture: two systems, one signal

The two halves of the project read the same molecule and do opposite things with it. System 1 reports the signal. System 2 removes the signal from circulation. They are designed to work together: the first indicates that an infection is present, and the second interferes with the coordination behind it.

Both are built in E. coli. The difference is what each cell is engineered to do with the signal: one converts it into fluorescence, and the other produces a molecule that binds it.

System 1 · Detect

Engineered E. coli that fluoresces when it hears AHL

E. coli carries the bacterium’s own receptor, LasR. When LasR binds the signal, it switches on a fluorescent reporter, so the presence of P. aeruginosa becomes something that can be measured.

  • Input — 3-oxo-C12-HSL, the Las system signal
  • Chassis — E. coli TOP10
  • Sensor — LasR, expressed constitutively from ProD with a strong RBS, so the receptor is always there to bind the signal
  • Output — sfGFP, from the LasR-dependent promoter pLasRV: signal bound, promoter on, cells fluoresce

The system inherits LasR’s selectivity and switch-like response, described below.[3][4] We also considered a structure-switching aptamer, which converts binding directly into fluorescence without expressing a protein,[7][8] and chose the receptor for the selectivity data behind it. The fluorescence is read without a plate reader, on the portable reader described on the Hardware page.

System 2 · Inhibit

Engineered E. coli that makes the aptamer

A DNA aptamer binds the AHL molecules themselves, so the signal never reaches its receptor and the population is not told to coordinate. Rather than synthesising the aptamer and adding it from outside, we have the cell make it: a retron writes the single-stranded DNA inside E. coli, and we extract it from the cells.

  • Target — the AHL signalling molecules, not the bacterium
  • Chassis — E. coli BL21(DE3)
  • Production — the retron Ec67, carried on pTwist. The aptamer sequence sits in the msd region, and the retron's own reverse transcriptase copies it into single-stranded DNA inside the cell
  • Recovery — the aptamer is produced inside the cells, and we extract it from them
  • Mechanism — competes with the receptor for the signal, interfering with quorum-sensing-coordinated behaviour[5][9][10]
  • Intended effect — weakened biofilm formation, reduced swarming motility, and less pyocyanin, a virulence factor

Aptamers raised against AHLs are reported not to kill bacteria or slow their growth.[5][9][10] This is intentional, and the reasoning is set out in “Signal capture versus antibiotic killing” below.

Rapid detection at the bedside

Reading the signal takes the wait out of the chain above. The sample does not have to be cultured, because the molecule is already there while the infection is building, so clinical staff can judge how likely a P. aeruginosa infection is on the same shift rather than two or three days later.

That is what makes the prescription something other than a guess. An answer early enough to act on means the antibiotic can be chosen for the organism, and the drugs held in reserve can be kept for the cases that need them. This is what the system is designed to do. What we have built and measured so far is on the Engineering page.

Signal capture versus antibiotic killing

An antibiotic kills bacteria, and in doing so it selects for the ones that survive. The next infection then starts from those survivors. This is the cycle behind antimicrobial resistance, and adding another compound that kills does not break it.

Removing the signal applies a different kind of pressure. Aptamers raised against AHLs are reported not to kill bacteria or slow their growth. Instead they bind the signalling molecules themselves, compete with the receptor, and interfere with quorum-sensing-coordinated behaviour.[5][9][10] Reported effects include suppression of swarming motility, up to 74.7% for one of the aptamers tested,[5] and inhibition of biofilm formation without a measurable effect on growth.[10]

We observed the same three behaviours in our own experiments. Against P. aeruginosa, Cb‑2, the aptamer our retron is built to produce, lowered swarming motility by 19.3%, pyocyanin production by 26.3% and biofilm formation by about 34%. We tested A16‑2 and ALS‑5 alongside it as reference aptamers, and the figures for all three are on the Results page.

The biofilm is a large part of what keeps a drug out. Cells growing in a biofilm are reported to become 10 to 1000 times more resistant to antimicrobial agents than the same cells growing free-living, through several mechanisms at once: the matrix delays penetration, the cells inside grow slowly, and some of them switch on a stress response.[11] Weakening the biofilm is therefore a way of allowing existing drugs to work again, rather than requiring new ones. This is what we are aiming at. It is our design rationale, not a result we have measured.
