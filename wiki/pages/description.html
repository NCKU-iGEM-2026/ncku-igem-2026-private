In an intensive care unit, a patient on a ventilator can develop pneumonia. Deciding whether they have, and which organism is behind it, currently takes longer than the decision about their antibiotics can wait.

We are building a cell-free biosensor that listens for the chemical signals bacteria use to talk to each other, and reports back within hours rather than days.

Why the answer arrives too late

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is diagnosed today by culturing sputum and by imaging. Other approaches have been tried. Each tells us something, and each stops short of what a bedside decision needs.

Sputum culture

Grows the organism from a sample.

Takes roughly 24–72 hours, cannot reflect the state of the infection in real time, and antibiotics already given can produce a false negative.

Breath analysis by GC–MS

Identifies volatile organic compounds in exhaled breath. A ventilator circuit model simulating the ICU showed both semi-invasive and non-invasive sampling could detect specific VOCs, though sampling accuracy depended on the molecular weight of the compound.[2]

GC–MS is laboratory-grade, large and precise — and cannot give a real-time answer at the bedside.[2]

Electronic nose

A portable alternative for reading the same volatile signature.

Reported at the time as lacking the sensitivity and specificity needed for clinical use in the ICU.[2]

Our approach: read what the bacteria are saying

Bacteria coordinate their behaviour by releasing and detecting small signalling molecules — quorum sensing. Those molecules are present before anything has been grown on a plate, and they are specific to the organism producing them. If we can detect the signal, we can detect the organism.

We want to do that in a cell-free system: the transcription and translation machinery taken out of the cell and run in vitro. A cell-free biosensor has already been operated on clinical sample types such as sputum, using a modular DNA-encoded sensor to detect the quorum sensing molecule 3-oxo-C12-HSL produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[3] That is the ground we are building on.

Which organism to start with

In Taiwan, the primary pathogens associated with VAP are Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae.[1]

We are starting with P. aeruginosa, because its quorum sensing circuit is the best characterised of the three and because both of the sensing routes we are considering already have published work against its signal.[3][5][6][9] A. baumannii is our second candidate: a screen of 77 synthetic AHLs against its AbaR receptor identified 3-OH-C12-HSL as the most likely natural quorum sensing signal of that organism.[4]

Why 3-oxo-C12-HSL is a good thing to detect

A diagnostic is only as good as its specificity. The receptor we would build around, LasR, is strongly selective for its own signal: it responds to 3-oxo-C12-HSL at sub-micromolar concentrations, while a short-chain AHL such as C4-HSL does not reach a measurable activation limit even at 100 µM — a difference of about two orders of magnitude.[5]

LasR also behaves usefully as a switch rather than a dial. It only forms a stable functional conformation after binding 3-oxo-C12-HSL, and then dimerises; that dimerisation gives positive cooperativity with a Hill coefficient of about 2, and together with positive feedback makes the las circuit bistable and hysteretic — a population-level switch rather than a gradual regulator.[6] For a diagnostic that has to say yes or no, a switch is what we want.

Work in a heterologous E. coli system supports the same picture: GFP reporter fusions were expressed far lower in a lasI deletion mutant, and the accumulation of 3-oxo-C12-HSL coincides closely with activation of lasI and rsaL.[12]

Two routes we are weighing

We have not fixed the sensing chemistry yet. Two routes are on the table, and they are not mutually exclusive.

Route A

A LasR genetic circuit, expressed cell-free

The signal is read by the bacterium's own receptor protein, produced in the cell-free reaction, which then drives a reporter.

  • Input — 3-oxo-C12-HSL
  • Sensor — LasR, from the constitutive promoter ProD with a strong RBS, using the Registry coding sequence BBa_C0079
  • Output — a reporter protein

Inherits the selectivity and switch-like response described above.[5][6] Full sequences will be documented on the Registry.

Route B

A structure-switching fluorescent aptamer

The signal is read by a DNA aptamer that changes shape when it binds, converting molecular recognition directly into a fluorescence change without needing protein expression at all.

  • A fluorophore-labelled strand is held quenched by a complementary quencher strand; the target displaces it and fluorescence returns.[7][8]
  • ALS-5 has been reported to detect 3-oxo-C12-HSL.[9]

Known trade-offs we would have to design around: structure switching can reduce the aptamer's target-binding affinity, and in the original ATP system a roughly sevenfold fluorescence enhancement took more than 30 minutes to reach its maximum at 37 °C.[7][8]

The idea we most want to test

If Route B works, the same molecule might do two jobs at once: report the infection, and blunt it.

Aptamers raised against AHLs appear not to kill bacteria or slow their growth; instead they bind the signalling molecules themselves, competing with the receptor and interfering with quorum-sensing-coordinated behaviour.[9][10][11] Reported effects include suppression of swarming motility — up to 74.7% for one of the aptamers tested[9] — and inhibition of biofilm formation without a measurable effect on growth.[11]

A diagnostic that also reduces virulence, without applying the selection pressure that drives resistance, is the outcome we would most like to reach. This is our hypothesis, not a result. We have not tested it.
