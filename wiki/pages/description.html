Pseudomonas aeruginosa is hard to treat not because any single cell is formidable, but because the cells act together. They coordinate by passing chemical messages between them, and what they coordinate is a defence: a biofilm that antibiotics struggle to cross.

Capture goes after the messages rather than the bacteria. Two systems, one signal: the first detects that signal and reports the infection, and the second captures it, so the coordination it was carrying never arrives.

Why P. aeruginosa

It is an opportunist that does its worst in exactly the places medicine cannot avoid creating: ventilators, catheters and open wounds. In the intensive care unit, where those are routine, the cost of a late answer is measured in days a patient does not have.

Figures our team has cited for the scale of this: an estimated 32,000 infections and 2,700 deaths a year in the United States citation to be completed; more than 140,000 patients affected each year in Taiwan citation to be completed; and mortality in the ICU reaching 20–40% citation to be completed.

In Taiwan, the primary pathogens associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia are Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae.[1] We start with P. aeruginosa because its quorum sensing circuit is the best characterised of the three, and because there is published work against its signal for both of the things we want to do — reading it, and binding it.[3][5][6][9] A. baumannii is our second candidate: a screen of 77 synthetic AHLs against its AbaR receptor identified 3-OH-C12-HSL as the most likely natural quorum sensing signal of that organism.[4]

The signal we are after

Bacteria coordinate their behaviour by releasing and detecting small signalling molecules — quorum sensing. In P. aeruginosa these are N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs). When enough cells are present, enough signal accumulates, and the population switches on the behaviours that only make sense in a crowd: virulence factors, and the biofilm.

That gives the signal two properties we can use. It is present early, before anything has been grown on a plate, and it is specific to the organism producing it. Detect the signal and you have detected the organism. Remove the signal and the coordination it was carrying does not happen.

Why the answer arrives too late

Infection in a ventilated patient is diagnosed today by culturing sputum and by imaging. Other approaches have been tried. Each tells us something, and each stops short of what a bedside decision needs.

Sputum culture Grows the organism from a sample.

Takes roughly 24–72 hours, cannot reflect the state of the infection in real time, and antibiotics already given can produce a false negative.

Breath analysis by GC–MS Identifies volatile organic compounds in exhaled breath.

A ventilator circuit model simulating the ICU showed both semi-invasive and non-invasive sampling could detect specific VOCs, though sampling accuracy depended on the molecular weight of the compound.[2] It is laboratory-grade, large and precise — and cannot give a real-time answer at the bedside.[2]

Electronic nose A portable alternative for reading the same volatile signature.

Reported at the time as lacking the sensitivity and specificity needed for clinical use in the ICU.[2]

Capture: two systems, one signal

The two halves of the project read the same molecule and do opposite things with it. System 1 lets the signal through and reports it. System 2 takes the signal out of circulation. They are designed to work together: the first says an infection is there, the second starts taking its coordination apart.

System 1 · Detect

Engineered E. coli that fluoresces when it hears AHL

The signal is read by the bacterium's own receptor protein, LasR, expressed in E. coli. Binding the signal switches on a fluorescent reporter, so the presence of P. aeruginosa becomes something you can see.

  • Input — 3-oxo-C12-HSL, the las system signal
  • Sensor — LasR, from the constitutive promoter ProD with a strong RBS, using the Registry coding sequence BBa_C0079
  • Output — fluorescence

Inherits the selectivity and switch-like response described below.[5][6] Full sequences will be documented on the Registry. We also considered reading the signal with a structure-switching aptamer, which turns binding straight into a fluorescence change without expressing a protein at all[7][8]; we went with the receptor for the selectivity data behind it.

System 2 · Inhibit

An aptamer that captures the signal

A DNA aptamer binds the AHL molecules themselves. A signal held by an aptamer is a signal that never reaches its receptor, so the population never gets the instruction to coordinate.

  • Target — the AHL signalling molecules, not the bacterium
  • Mechanism — competes with the receptor for the signal, interfering with quorum-sensing-coordinated behaviour[9][10][11]
  • Intended effect — weakened biofilm formation

Aptamers raised against AHLs are reported not to kill bacteria or slow their growth.[9][10][11] That is the point: see “Why capturing beats killing” below.

Why 3-oxo-C12-HSL is a good thing to detect

A diagnostic is only as good as its specificity. The receptor System 1 is built around, LasR, is strongly selective for its own signal: it responds to 3-oxo-C12-HSL at sub-micromolar concentrations, while a short-chain AHL such as C4-HSL does not reach a measurable activation limit even at 100 µM — a difference of about two orders of magnitude.[5]

LasR also behaves usefully as a switch rather than a dial. It only forms a stable functional conformation after binding 3-oxo-C12-HSL, and then dimerises; that dimerisation gives positive cooperativity with a Hill coefficient of about 2, and together with positive feedback makes the las circuit bistable and hysteretic — a population-level switch rather than a gradual regulator.[6] For a diagnostic that has to say yes or no, a switch is what we want.

Work in a heterologous E. coli system supports the same picture: GFP reporter fusions were expressed far lower in a lasI deletion mutant, and the accumulation of 3-oxo-C12-HSL coincides closely with activation of lasI and rsaL.[12] That is the same host System 1 is built in.

Detecting 3-oxo-C12-HSL in a clinical sample is not without precedent: a modular DNA-encoded sensor has been used to detect it on sample types such as sputum.[3] That is the ground we are building on.

Why capturing beats killing

An antibiotic kills, and in killing it selects: the survivors are the ones that resisted, and the next infection starts from them. That is the cycle behind antimicrobial resistance, and adding another compound that kills does not break it.

Taking the signal away is a different kind of pressure. Aptamers raised against AHLs appear not to kill bacteria or slow their growth; instead they bind the signalling molecules themselves, competing with the receptor and interfering with quorum-sensing-coordinated behaviour.[9][10][11] Reported effects include suppression of swarming motility — up to 74.7% for one of the aptamers tested[9] — and inhibition of biofilm formation without a measurable effect on growth.[11]

If the biofilm is what keeps a drug out, then weakening it is a way of letting existing drugs work again rather than demanding new ones. That is what we are aiming at. It is our design rationale, not a result we have measured.
