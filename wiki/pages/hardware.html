CAPTURE-Screen is a single-cuvette fluorescence reader for GFP reporter cultures. It measures their green fluorescence directly at the bench, beside the incubator, without waiting for a shared plate reader, and we built it for our own AHL biosensor. We developed two versions of the device: a 3D-printed version and a laser-cut version, each housed in its own enclosure. The following sections describe the features shared by both designs, followed by a detailed overview of each version.

{# ========================= why build one, and for what assay ========================= #}

Background

A GFP reporter is read as green light from a liquid culture, usually on a shared plate reader. Ours reports AHL, the quorum-sensing signal, and its assay set what this reader had to do: a small sample, read every hour, a comparison with a plate reader, and an instrument cheap enough to rebuild.

200 µL
of sfGFP culture per reaction The reporter is superfolder GFP.
Every hour
for six hours, at 37 °C A shared plate reader is booked, scheduled and in one building. Either build can sit next to the incubator.
485 / 510
nm, the plate-reader benchmark Ours excites at 470 nm, and its geometry, drive current, integration time and dark baseline are ours to state.
21–65×
cheaper than an open-source plate reader At most: US$161 for the 3D-printed build and US$54 for the laser-cut one, by our own costing, against under US$3,500 for the plate reader of Szymula and colleagues5. Anyone with the source files can rebuild ours.

The need is not only ours. Green fluorescence from a GFP reporter in a liquid sample is among the commonest readings in synthetic biology: one interlaboratory study had 72 iGEM teams measure it in E. coli, on plate readers and flow cytometers6. Cell-free reactions, developed as portable sensors7, hold no cells at all. The reader is meant for samples like these: liquid, and not too dense. We tested the effect of sample density on the 3D-printed build. Both builds have read sfGFP in E. coli TOP10 culture, and both have read our AHL sensor strain, which neither could tell from cells without sfGFP (the laser-cut build’s test).

{# ========================= how it measures ========================= #}

Measurement principle

Both readers keep the blue that excites the sample apart from the green it gives off in two ways: by angle, and by colour. Neither uses an emission filter: the sorting by colour is the sensor's own.

{# ========================= how we got here ========================= #}

How we got here

We built it for our biosensor, rehearsed on GFP cultures, and ended with a platform that other teams can tune to their own samples.

We expected an AHL-induced culture to be about as bright as a GFP culture, so the first stage ran on a GFP test strain as a rehearsal. What we had read beforehand put the two in the same range, as one LuxR circuit is8. What we had not noticed is that circuits differ: a Las device, the kind our biosensor is, stayed well below a constitutive reference9.

Along the way we found that how dense a sample is changes the reading, and worked on taking that out with a model and with the reader’s other channels (Model 3.5).

The samples our wet lab then gave us were denser still, and AHL-dependent induction had not been shown conclusively (Engineering): not what we had prepared for, and a different thing to measure. A plate reader has its place, and we read ours beside one. What the two builds offer is a steady platform to read from. Both record ten channels on every read, and both run from the same software. Under its shield the 3D-printed build reads zero in the dark, so its counts are used as they come; the laser-cut build is cheaper and easier to assemble, and removes its background in the data. A team can choose which bands to read, and how to process them, to suit its own samples.
