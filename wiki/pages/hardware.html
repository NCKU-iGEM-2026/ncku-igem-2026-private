Background

A plate reader answers the question we are asking, so the argument for building our own instrument is not that the measurement is impossible without it. The argument is about who can make the measurement, where, and at what cost.

Cost and access
A shared benchtop reader is booked, scheduled and located in one building. An instrument that costs a few thousand NT dollars in parts can sit next to the incubator, and can be rebuilt by anyone with the source files.
A measurement we can defend
We can state our own optical geometry, drive current, integration time and dark baseline, because we chose them. That makes a result traceable in a way that a black-box instrument setting is not.
An engineering object to test
Building it forces every assumption into the open: stray light, alignment, repeatability. Most of this page exists because building the thing produced questions that a plate reader would have hidden.

The plate reader has not been discarded. It remains our benchmark: the wet lab's reference condition is excitation 485 nm, emission 510 nm at 200 µL, and CAPTURE-Screen's job is to be comparable against it, not to replace it before it has earned the right.

The instrument

CAPTURE-Screen P1 is a single-cuvette reader with a fixed optical core. Excitation enters along one axis; emission is collected at 90° to it. Everything the excitation does not deposit in the sample is absorbed by a light trap on the far side rather than allowed to find its way to the detector.

What the detector actually saw

The optical core can be described from the drawing, but the only thing that settles whether it works is what the detector reported with the instrument in a known state. The bench below is that record, made steerable: pick a state of the lamp, of the sample position and of the room, and it shows the channel counts from the session that measured it.

It is a record and not a model. Where a combination was never measured, it says so rather than interpolating a plausible-looking number.

Ten channels instead of one filter

A conventional fluorimeter puts a narrow emission filter in front of a single photodiode, and everything outside the emission band is thrown away in glass. The AS7341 does something else. It carries eight interference-filtered channels spread across the visible range plus a clear and a near-infrared channel, so one read returns the shape of the light arriving at the detector rather than a single number.

That shape is what makes the measurements on this page checkable. When excitation reached the sample for the first time, the detector did not merely register light — it registered light concentrated in F2 and F3, the two channels that bracket a 470 nm source. A blue source producing a green-looking spectrum would have meant something was wrong with the optics or the sensor, and the channel shape would have said so at once.

AS7341 channel map

Eight filtered channels at nominal centres of 415, 445, 480, 515, 555, 590, 630 and 680 nm, plus an unfiltered Clear channel and a near-infrared channel. Excitation is nominally 470 nm and sits between F2 and F3; sfGFP emission is read on F4 at 515 nm.

Working settings in the fluorescence sessions: AGAIN 512×, ATIME 255, ASTEP 280. Centre wavelengths are the sensor's nominal channel centres.

The instrument has to be shielded

Ambient rejection is the sharpest operating constraint on the build. With the LED de-energised in a darkened room, every channel reads zero, and holds at zero all the way out to 25× integration. Under normal room lighting with no shield it does not: an empty UVette in the holder gives F4 = 11 counts with the lamp off.

Two leak paths were separated by measuring with and without the cuvette under identical lighting. The mechanism itself contributes 4 counts. The remaining 7 come from the UVette acting as a light pipe: it is transparent, it sits in an open +Z slot, and it carries room light down into the measurement region. Putting an opaque box over the instrument returns every channel to zero.

The leak is also not a constant that can be subtracted away. It scales non-proportionally with integration time, which is what lamp flicker aliasing against the integration window looks like, so a dark-frame subtraction does not remove it. Hence the operating rule: measure in a darkened room or under an opaque shield, never in open room light.

A free quality check falls out of this. Because a correctly shielded dark read returns exactly zero, any non-zero pre-dark reading is direct evidence that shielding was not restored for that measurement. We treat such a record as invalid rather than correcting it — the instrument tells you when you have made a mistake, without needing a separate calibration step.

It is not theoretical. During the September calibration session the firmware flagged one acquisition cycle on its own: the pre-dark near-infrared channel read 1 where every other channel read 0, almost certainly light entering while the cuvette was being re-seated. That cycle was dropped and the raw row kept. The other two cycles of the same insertion agreed with the two insertions either side of it.

A bench circuit, deliberately boring

The excitation branch does one job: turn a single LED on and off at a current that cannot damage it, and be provably off when the microcontroller is not driving it. It is a resistor-limited LED in series with an N-channel MOSFET on the low side, switched from one GPIO. There is no constant-current driver, no PWM dimming and no PCB — this is a breadboard topology that was measured, not a product design.

The one part that is not optional is R3, the gate pulldown. Without it the gate floats while the ESP32 boots or while it is unpowered, and the LED can emit during what the firmware believes is a dark baseline. That turns a safety detail into a data-integrity detail.

Excitation branch and sensor bus

Hover or focus a labelled node for its measured value

Measured 2026-08-25 on breadboard. Total draw ≈ 77 mA. ILED = 5.553 mA — a safe-start value, not the operating current.

Why the current is deliberately low. The nominal design target is 8–12 mA; the bench branch runs at 5.553 mA. That gap is not an oversight and it has not been closed, because closing it requires knowing how much signal is enough — and signal-to-noise, limit of detection and the expected negative and positive range are all still open. Raising the drive current without that basis would be guessing.

Acquisition timing

One reading is not instantaneous. The LED turns on, the optical and sensor state is given time to settle, the sensor integrates across two SMUX configurations to cover all channels, and the LED turns off again. The firmware holds a hard 1.0 s ceiling on that window.

Decisions that shaped P1

Each of these is a recorded, binding project decision from the 2026-08-09 batch. Several of them removed capability from the prototype, and a reader who only saw the final scope would not know that the narrowing was deliberate. The ones that did are marked.

Turbidity QC is out of P1 acceptance narrows P1
Red-light transmission and turbidity QC are removed. The far side of the excitation axis is occupied by the light trap, so there is no transmission path and the measurement is not physically implementable in this geometry.
470 nm excitation for the first round
Three independent design documents agree on the nominal 470 nm Cree LED, and the LED holder was dimensioned from that part's datasheet.
The UVette is a benchmark, not the product vessel
The reaction cartridge is a joint wet–dry decision that has not been made. Future containers will be handled with adapters rather than by modifying approved optical modules.
PlateDock is out of P1 scope narrows P1
Petri-dish imaging and swarming analysis are held as separate future work. No CAD for it exists in any revision, and P1's resources belong on validating one reader properly.
Angled-wedge light trap selected for manufacture
The stepped-baffle variant is deferred, not rejected. Both validate digitally and no bench evidence separates them, so the selection is a manufacturing choice and explicitly not a claim that the alternative is worse.

The wet–dry interface

The reader has to fit an assay that the wet lab defines. These are the handoff facts we are designing against; where something is still unknown we have left it unknown rather than assuming a value.

Our nominal 470 nm excitation is deliberately distinct from the plate reader's 485 nm, so the two instruments are comparable but not identical — a difference we will have to characterise rather than assume away. Negative-control definition, replicate counts and the expected fluorescence range are still to come from the wet lab, and no traceable raw plate-reader dataset is available yet.
