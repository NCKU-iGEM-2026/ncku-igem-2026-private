Our wet lab builds a cell-free reporter that answers an acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) signal by producing sfGFP. Reading that answer normally means a benchtop plate reader — an instrument that is neither cheap nor portable, and that we do not control.

CAPTURE-Screen is our own reader for it: a low-cost, research-use 90° fluorescence instrument built around a single cuvette. This page describes the first physical prototype, P1-PROTO-01, what it achieved at first light on 2026‑08‑25, and — in as much detail as the successes — what it has not yet been shown to do.

A note on the name. Earlier internal documents call this instrument CAPTURE-Reader. That name is superseded; the project is CAPTURE-Screen throughout. Where an older file is cited here it is cited under its historical name.

Why build a reader at all

A plate reader answers the question we are asking, so the argument for building our own instrument is not that the measurement is impossible without it. The argument is about who can make the measurement, where, and at what cost.

Cost and access
A shared benchtop reader is booked, scheduled and located in one building. An instrument that costs a few thousand NT dollars in parts can sit next to the incubator, and can be rebuilt by anyone with the source files.
A measurement we can defend
We can state our own optical geometry, drive current, integration time and dark baseline, because we chose them. That makes a result traceable in a way that a black-box instrument setting is not.
An engineering object to test
Building it forces every assumption into the open: stray light, alignment, repeatability. Most of this page exists because building the thing produced questions that a plate reader would have hidden.

The plate reader has not been discarded. It remains our benchmark: the wet lab's reference condition is excitation 485 nm, emission 510 nm at 200 µL, and CAPTURE-Screen's job is to be comparable against it, not to replace it before it has earned the right.

The instrument

CAPTURE-Screen P1 is a single-cuvette reader with a fixed optical core. Excitation enters along one axis; emission is collected at 90° to it. Everything the excitation does not deposit in the sample is absorbed by a light trap on the far side rather than allowed to find its way to the detector.

Decisions that shaped P1

Each of these is a recorded, binding project decision. We list them because several of them removed capability from the prototype, and a reader who only saw the final scope would not know that the narrowing was deliberate.

Two fractures, and the decision to keep them

Two parts fractured during support removal. Both were bonded with opaque black electrical tape and kept in service.

  • The LED holder body split into two halves at the C‑clip slot plane, so the slot now straddles a bonded joint.
  • The aperture baffle body fractured at both aperture-disk pocket planes, leaving three pieces. The LED holder therefore locates against a bonded fragment rather than a monolithic part.

Counting the LED holder's own joint, the excitation axis passes through three bonded joints in series, and angular error at any one of them propagates directly to excitation aim. The decision on 2026-08-24 was not to reprint: the effect of the joints would be assessed at first light rather than pre-empted by a speculative reprint, with the three-piece baffle named in advance as the first suspect if alignment proved unsatisfactory.

Both fractures occurred at pocket or slot planes that remove a large fraction of the cross-section at a single layer boundary in the as-printed orientation. That is a design lesson for the next revision, and it is recorded as one.

First light, 2026-08-25

The assembled P1-PROTO-01 was measured against the watch list written before it was switched on. Each result below is reported with the boundary of what it covers, because several of them are easy to over-read.

  1. Result 1 The optical chain works With a scatterer at the sample position the detector registered the excitation: F2 (445 nm) = 87, F3 (480 nm) = 81, Clear = 116, against all-zero dark reads. Excitation reaches the sample, the detection path is open, and 90° collection works — through three bonded joints.
  2. Result 2 Ambient light is rejected Closed, with the room lit, all twelve channels read exactly 0 with the LED off — at 25× the working integration time. Ambient contributes below roughly 0.04 counts at working settings.
  3. Result 3 Bare-path stray light is below 1 count LED energised, nothing at the sample position: every channel reads 0. The light trap and baffle absorb the excitation essentially completely, bonded joints included.
  4. Result 4 Position holds, once settled After a one-time settling of about −6.9 % that converged, three undisturbed repetitions gave a run-to-run spread of 0.42 %. A light touch on the LED cable moved the reading by 0.28 % — 0.46 pooled standard deviations, i.e. indistinguishable from noise.

Read result 3 carefully. With a water blank and no emission filter fitted, the excitation leakage into the emission channel is F4/F3 < 1.35 %. That figure is an upper bound set by the quantisation floor — the channel read zero, so we can only say the leakage is smaller than one count, not what it is. It was also measured at 1600 µL rather than the 200 µL working volume, where the meniscus sits nearer the beam and the result may be worse. And a real culture carries turbidity, which will scatter far more than pure water.

Electronics, measured on the bench

The excitation branch is a resistor-limited, low-side-switched LED on a single ESP32 GPIO, with the detector on the board's 3.3 V rail and one named ground junction. Staged bring-up passed at every stage.

One component carries a caveat worth stating. The single 2N7000 switch has conditional acceptance only: its pinout was established by body-diode measurement rather than assumed, and its manufacturer is unknown — the part is marked and lot-coded but carries no logo. That licenses no substitution and is not a design approval.

What we have not measured

The most important sentence on this page is this one: no fluorescence of any kind has been measured. No fluorophore was available on 2026-08-25. Everything above establishes that the instrument's optical and electrical paths function; none of it establishes that the instrument can measure the thing it was built to measure.

A digital CAD or DFM pass is not evidence of printing, fit, assembly, stray-light performance, fluorescence, or repeatability. We hold those apart deliberately, and the tables above are written so that a reader can see exactly where the evidence stops.

What we are and are not claiming

CAPTURE-Screen is a research-use (RUO) prototype. Its stable claim is deliberately narrow, and we hold to it in every document:

The device reads relative biosensor fluorescence and flags optical interference.

It does not, and we do not claim that it does:

  • diagnose ventilator-associated pneumonia, or any other condition;
  • directly detect or quantify Pseudomonas aeruginosa;
  • measure bacterial concentration;
  • quantify AHL.

The sample container is not final, the emission filter is not chosen, and the reader has never seen a fluorophore. Those are facts about where the project stands in August 2026, not caveats added for form's sake.

The wet–dry interface

The reader has to fit an assay that the wet lab defines. These are the handoff facts we are designing against; where something is still unknown we have left it unknown rather than assuming a value.

Reporter
sfGFP. The exact construct and strain are not yet specified.
AHL series
10 µM, 1 µM, 100 nM, 10 nM, 1 nM.
Volume
200 µL per reaction.
Incubation
37 °C, read every hour for six hours. Whether the series includes a zero point is not yet settled.
Benchmark
Plate reader at excitation 485 nm, emission 510 nm, 200 µL.

Our nominal 470 nm excitation is deliberately distinct from the plate reader's 485 nm, so the two instruments are comparable but not identical — a difference we will have to characterise rather than assume away. Negative-control definition, replicate counts and the expected fluorescence range are still to come from the wet lab, and no traceable raw plate-reader dataset is available yet.

What happens next

  1. Obtain a fluorescent reference standard, and measure a real signal. Everything else waits on this.
  2. Derive the emission-filter requirement from the measured signal-to-background ratio, rather than guessing at it.
  3. Test UVette re-insertion repeatability across a six-hour series — the open item most likely to spoil a timed run.
  4. Re-measure stray light at the working 200 µL volume, and with a turbid sample.
  5. Implement and flash the project firmware, replacing the diagnostic sketches used for bring-up.
  6. Run the reader and the plate reader against the same AHL series, and characterise the difference between them.

Every measurement quoted on this page is drawn from our own dated engineering records, each of which names the session and the evidence file that produced it. Where a value is an upper bound, a safe-start setting, or a scoped pass, we have said so rather than rounding it into a capability.
