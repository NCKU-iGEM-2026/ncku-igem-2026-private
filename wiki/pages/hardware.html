Background

A plate reader answers the question we are asking, so the argument for building our own instrument is not that the measurement is impossible without it. The argument is about who can make the measurement, where, and at what cost.

Cost and access
A shared benchtop reader is booked, scheduled and located in one building. An instrument that costs a few thousand NT dollars in parts can sit next to the incubator, and can be rebuilt by anyone with the source files.
A measurement we can defend
We can state our own optical geometry, drive current, integration time and dark baseline, because we chose them. That makes a result traceable in a way that a black-box instrument setting is not.
An engineering object to test
Building it forces every assumption into the open: stray light, alignment, repeatability. Most of this page exists because building the thing produced questions that a plate reader would have hidden.

The plate reader has not been discarded. It remains our benchmark: the wet lab's reference condition is excitation 485 nm, emission 510 nm at 200 µL, and CAPTURE-Screen's job is to be comparable against it, not to replace it before it has earned the right.

The instrument

CAPTURE-Screen P1 is a single-cuvette reader with a fixed optical core. Excitation enters along one axis; emission is collected at 90° to it. Everything the excitation does not deposit in the sample is absorbed by a light trap on the far side rather than allowed to find its way to the detector.

What the detector actually saw

The optical core can be described from the drawing, but the only thing that settles whether it works is what the detector reported with the instrument in a known state. The bench below is that record, made steerable: pick a state of the lamp, of the sample position and of the room, and it shows the channel counts from the session that measured it.

It is a record and not a model. Where a combination was never measured, it says so rather than interpolating a plausible-looking number.

Ten channels instead of one filter

A conventional fluorimeter puts a narrow emission filter in front of a single photodiode, and everything outside the emission band is thrown away in glass. The AS7341 does something else. It carries eight interference-filtered channels spread across the visible range plus a clear and a near-infrared channel, so one read returns the shape of the light arriving at the detector rather than a single number.

That shape is what makes the measurements on this page checkable. When excitation reached the sample for the first time, the detector did not merely register light — it registered light concentrated in F2 and F3, the two channels that bracket a 470 nm source. A blue source producing a green-looking spectrum would have meant something was wrong with the optics or the sensor, and the channel shape would have said so at once.

The instrument has to be shielded

Ambient rejection is the sharpest operating constraint on the build. With the LED de-energised in a darkened room, every channel reads zero, and holds at zero all the way out to 25× integration. Under normal room lighting with no shield it does not: an empty UVette in the holder gives F4 = 11 counts with the lamp off.

Two leak paths were separated by measuring with and without the cuvette under identical lighting. The mechanism itself contributes 4 counts. The remaining 7 come from the UVette acting as a light pipe: it is transparent, it sits in an open +Z slot, and it carries room light down into the measurement region. Putting an opaque box over the instrument returns every channel to zero.

The leak is also not a constant that can be subtracted away. It scales non-proportionally with integration time, which is what lamp flicker aliasing against the integration window looks like, so a dark-frame subtraction does not remove it. Hence the operating rule: measure in a darkened room or under an opaque shield, never in open room light.

A free quality check falls out of this. Because a correctly shielded dark read returns exactly zero, any non-zero pre-dark reading is direct evidence that shielding was not restored for that measurement. We treat such a record as invalid rather than correcting it — the instrument tells you when you have made a mistake, without needing a separate calibration step.

It is not theoretical. During the September calibration session the firmware flagged one acquisition cycle on its own: the pre-dark near-infrared channel read 1 where every other channel read 0, almost certainly light entering while the cuvette was being re-seated. That cycle was dropped and the raw row kept. The other two cycles of the same insertion agreed with the two insertions either side of it.

A bench circuit, deliberately boring

The excitation branch does one job: turn a single LED on and off at a current that cannot damage it, and be provably off when the microcontroller is not driving it. It is a resistor-limited LED in series with an N-channel MOSFET on the low side, switched from one GPIO. There is no constant-current driver, no PWM dimming and no PCB — this is a breadboard topology that was measured, not a product design.

The one part that is not optional is R3, the gate pulldown. Without it the gate floats while the ESP32 boots or while it is unpowered, and the LED can emit during what the firmware believes is a dark baseline. That turns a safety detail into a data-integrity detail.

Excitation branch and sensor bus

Measured 2026-08-25 on breadboard. Total draw ≈ 77 mA. ILED = 5.553 mA — a safe-start value, not the operating current.

Why the current is deliberately low. The nominal design target is 8–12 mA; the bench branch runs at 5.553 mA. That gap is not an oversight and it has not been closed, because closing it requires knowing how much signal is enough — and signal-to-noise, limit of detection and the expected negative and positive range are all still open. Raising the drive current without that basis would be guessing.

Acquisition timing

One reading is not instantaneous. The LED turns on, the optical and sensor state is given time to settle, the sensor integrates across two SMUX configurations to cover all channels, and the LED turns off again. The firmware holds a hard 1.0 s ceiling on that window.

Does the reading track the reporter, or just the cells?

Every fluorescence reading before September carried the same unanswered question. A tube of culture is both fluorescent and cloudy. If the number goes up, is that more reporter protein — or simply more cells in the beam? And if the cells scatter and absorb light on the way out, do they suppress the very signal they produce?

The session of 2026-09-16 was built to separate those two things. Two stock cultures were used, one reported to carry the GFP reporter and one not. Preparations were mixed across two independent axes: d, the total dilution of culture into medium, and f, the fraction of that culture drawn from the GFP stock. Their product G = d × f is how much GFP stock is in the cuvette; d on its own is how much cell material is in it. Vary them independently and the two effects come apart.

Everything ran at the assay volume — 200 µL in a lab cuvette, ORIENT-A, shielded, three acquisition cycles each, firmware P1-ACQ-1.0.0 at gate 1. All 73 serial logs were kept unfiltered, including the boot messages and the commands the firmware refused.

What the model resolves, and what it does not

The analysis model was written down in the design document before the session ran, so the result is a test rather than a search. It fits the emission channel as a baseline, a term for GFP content, a term for total cell content, and an interaction term that asks whether cells suppress the signal. One coefficient comes out clearly. The rest do not — and the honest reading of this session is mostly in that second sentence.

Coefficient estimates, with 95 % intervals

F4 = β₀ + β₁·G + β₂·G·d + β₃·d · 14 preparations, 10 degrees of freedom

Baseline −2.38 ± 1.64; GFP content +38.28 ± 6.27; turbidity interaction −8.81 ± 7.23; cell content alone +2.81 ± 2.47 (standard errors). Only the GFP term has an interval clear of zero.

Residual standard deviation 1.68 counts · R² 0.976 β₁ and β₂ are correlated at −0.96, so they cannot be separated cleanly

The emission channel tracks GFP content, and the relationship is close to linear. Each unit of GFP stock fraction is worth about 38 counts, with a residual scatter of 1.68 counts — small against a signal range of 0 to 30. Adding a squared term to test for curvature returns essentially nothing, and substituting the reported optical density for the dilution axis changes no conclusion.

Whether the cells suppress that signal is not answerable from this session. The interaction term's interval runs from −24.9 to +7.3, which in relative terms means the slope could fall by 65 % as turbidity goes from nothing to full strength, or rise by 19 %. Both are compatible with the data. We can neither claim that turbidity matters nor rule out that it matters a great deal.

This session should have stopped before it started. The design required the two stock cultures to be matched in concentration first, so that the dilution axis would mean the same thing for both. Measured on arrival, the GFP stock read an optical density of 1.56 against 0.98 for the non-GFP stock — a factor of about 2.6. Under the design's own stopping rule, that difference meant the matrix should not be opened. It was run anyway, knowingly, and the whole session is labelled unmatched as a result. That is why d, f and G cannot be read as cell counts or protein amounts, and it is the single biggest reason the turbidity axis came out unresolvable.

Repeatability by channel

Coefficient of variation · lower is better

F4 across three re-insertions of one cuvette, 5.5 %; across three independent preparations, 7.4 %. Clear across three preparations with cells, 38 %; across three no-GFP preparations, 94 %; across six empty cuvettes, 97 %.

Six empty cuvettes

Same instrument, same settings, nothing inside

Clear counts from six empty cuvettes: 1.67, 3.67, 5.00, 0.00, 3.00 and 11.67. Every one of them read F4 = 0.

Those two figures are the most practically useful thing the session produced. The emission channel is reproducible — about 5 % across three re-insertions of the same cuvette, about 7 % across independently mixed preparations of the same recipe. That is the number to carry into assay design.

The scattering channels are not. Six empty cuvettes, with nothing in them at all, returned Clear readings from 0.00 to 11.67 — a spread as large as the entire range the cells produce. The cuvette, not the sample, dominates those channels at these light levels. An apparent turbidity trend in the no-GFP series survives or disappears depending on which two cuvettes are included, so it is not a real result.

And one number recurs everywhere: every empty cuvette, the medium blank, and every single sample containing no GFP read exactly zero on the emission channel. Zero is a legitimate reading here, not a detection floor and not missing data — worth stating because the automated analysis pipeline currently discards those rows, which is why this session was analysed from the raw logs instead.

Recorded deviations from the plan

Five departures from the written design were logged during the session. They are listed here rather than buried, because three of them change how the results may be read.

Stock matching was skipped entirelyChanges how the results read

The design required the two stock cultures to be brought to the same concentration before any mixing, with a stopping rule if they could not be matched. That step was not performed; the stocks were used as received and the session opened straight onto the first sample. Measured display optical densities were 1.56 for the GFP stock and 0.98 for the non-GFP stock, and at a common 25 % dilution 0.71 against 0.27. The whole session is therefore labelled unmatched, and the mixing fractions cannot be read as cell counts.

The opening blank was moved to the endMinor

The design placed a 200 µL dilution-medium blank at the start of the session. It was run at the end instead. It read zero on all four reported channels.

One re-preparation was prompted by its readingChanges how the results read

A mid-session rule was agreed: re-prepare a sample only when the cuvette is visibly marked before filling, never because the number came out high. One re-test met that rule — the cuvette was visibly dirty. A second did not: it was re-prepared because its reading matched the dirty-cuvette pattern. That biases the no-GFP series toward low readings, and it is the reason the apparent turbidity trend in that series is not treated as a result.

One acquisition cycle was voided by the firmwareWorking as intended

During the second insertion of one sample the firmware flagged AMBIENT_PRESENT on the first cycle: the pre-dark near-infrared channel read 1 where every other pre-dark channel read 0, most likely a light leak while the cuvette was being re-seated. That row was excluded from the average and kept in the raw log. The other two cycles of that insertion agreed with the two insertions either side of it.

One sample was measured three times overPlanned

One recipe was deliberately re-inserted three times to separate cuvette-handling variation from preparation variation. That comparison is what produced the 5 % and 7 % repeatability figures above.

None of this changes the project's test status. The session record is explicit that its canonical impact is nil: no test row moves, no procurement or release state changes, and firmware hardware validation stays where it was. Session evidence becomes project status only through a separate synchronisation step with its own review.

What has actually been tested

Four rules govern every row. A pass applies only to the scope written next to it. A digital validation pass says nothing about printing, fit, assembly or optical behaviour. Repeated acquisition cycles within one insertion are not independent samples. And a test that was executed but not accepted is not a pass.

A conditional pass names the condition it depends on; an unknown means no record exists either way. A filter cassette is not a filter, a reference model in CAD is not a purchased component, and a part in the model is not a part that was bought, received, installed or tested. Those distinctions are maintained in the project record, and this page inherits them.

Decisions that shaped P1

Each of these is a recorded, binding project decision from the 2026-08-09 batch. Several of them removed capability from the prototype, and a reader who only saw the final scope would not know that the narrowing was deliberate. The ones that did are marked.

Turbidity QC is out of P1 acceptance narrows P1
Red-light transmission and turbidity QC are removed. The far side of the excitation axis is occupied by the light trap, so there is no transmission path and the measurement is not physically implementable in this geometry.
470 nm excitation for the first round
Three independent design documents agree on the nominal 470 nm Cree LED, and the LED holder was dimensioned from that part's datasheet.
The UVette is a benchmark, not the product vessel
The reaction cartridge is a joint wet–dry decision that has not been made. Future containers will be handled with adapters rather than by modifying approved optical modules.
PlateDock is out of P1 scope narrows P1
Petri-dish imaging and swarming analysis are held as separate future work. No CAD for it exists in any revision, and P1's resources belong on validating one reader properly.
Angled-wedge light trap selected for manufacture
The stepped-baffle variant is deferred, not rejected. Both validate digitally and no bench evidence separates them, so the selection is a manufacturing choice and explicitly not a claim that the alternative is worse.

The reader has to fit an assay that the wet lab defines. These are the handoff facts we are designing against; where something is still unknown we have left it unknown rather than assuming a value.

Negative-control definition, replicate counts and the expected fluorescence range are still to come from the wet lab, and no traceable raw plate-reader dataset is available yet.

What changed when we took it outside the team

Between July and August the enclosure went through a series of materials and designs, and most of those prototypes failed on strength or on fabrication time. On 10 September 2026 we took the design files to Ai Wei Li Laser Engraving Studio and worked through them with the manufacturer.

Three things came back that we had not accounted for. Materials differ in how they reflect light, and that has to be part of choosing one for an optical enclosure rather than an afterthought. Wall thickness matters most at the joints, because a badly made joint is where light leaks in. And the assembly and adjustment were done at the studio with the manufacturer present, which is how the two-layer shell we now have came to be built the way it is.

The full write-up, and the rest of the people we consulted, are on the Human Practices page.

This is expert consultation that changed the build. It is not the same thing as putting the finished instrument in front of the people who would use it and recording what they found, and we are not presenting it as that.

Reproducing it

The instrument is built from catalogue parts and printed components. Everything a second team needs falls into three groups: the parts to buy, the files to print and flash, and the settings to run it at. The first and the third are below and on this page; the second is listed in the manifest underneath, with its current state stated plainly.

Quantities and prices are being reconciled against our final order records and are deliberately omitted until they are. An estimate that later turns out wrong is worse for a team costing a rebuild than no number at all.
