{% if part == 'detect' and cycle == 2 and stage == 'test' %}

To evaluate whether the promoter modification improved biosensor performance, we repeated the experimental approaches used in the first cycle, including direct supplementation with synthetic AHL, co-culture with PAO1, and agar diffusion assays.

However, the redesigned biosensor exhibited a similar response pattern to the original construct. Detectable fluorescence was still limited to the agar-based assay using PAO1 culture supernatant, while the other experimental conditions failed to produce the expected fluorescence response.

These results suggested that the genetic modifications were insufficient to overcome the limitations observed in our initial experiments.

To further analyze the fluorescence response, we collaborated with our Dry Lab team to integrate an image-processing software tool capable of identifying fluorescent regions on agar plates: the plate-photo analysis in LasReader, which finds the plate, outlines the bright region on it and reports its size in millimetres. This approach allowed us to assess the spatial distribution of fluorescence and explore a more objective method for interpreting our biosensor’s output, providing a foundation for future integration with our hardware detection platform. (Fig. D10)

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 2 and stage == 'learn' %}

Despite modifying the promoter controlling LasR expression and the LasR sequence itself, we did not observe a substantial improvement in fluorescence. Although we could not rule out problems with our genetic circuit, these results encouraged us to investigate other possible factors.

We began by reconsidering the stability of AHL molecules. Previous research has shown that AHLs can undergo pH- and temperature-dependent lactonolysis, potentially reducing their biological activity.[4] In addition, PAO1 culture supernatant contains various metabolites besides AHL, which might influence our biosensor’s response. Differences between liquid culture and agar plates, including signal diffusion and bacterial growth conditions, could also contribute to the inconsistent results.

Another possibility was the difference between cell-free and living-cell systems. Our initial design was based on previous cell-free biosensor research, but the behavior of a genetic circuit may change when introduced into living bacteria. Although Chappell et al.[5] demonstrated that many regulatory elements show comparable performance in both systems, differences in their biological environments may still influence gene expression.

Since we could not identify the exact cause of our unsuccessful experiments, we decided to revisit the literature and repeat our initial synthetic AHL assay under more carefully controlled conditions, following the experimental approach described by Chappell et al.[5]

Following the redesigned genetic circuit, we constructed a new plasmid in which the original ProD promoter was replaced with J23101 and the LasR also modified to unoptimized one, while retaining the LasR-responsive pLasRV promoter and downstream sfGFP reporter. (Fig. D7)

The redesigned plasmid, pTwist-J23101-LasR; LasRV-sfGFP, was introduced into E. coli TOP10 for subsequent fluorescence testing. (Fig. D8)

Whole-cell lysates of the rebuilt J23101–LasR construct were then separated by SDS-PAGE beside the ProD–LasR construct from the first cycle, both uninduced and without AHL, three lanes each. (Fig. D9)

Based on the findings from our previous engineering cycles, we decided to revisit our initial experimental approach using synthetic 3-oxo-C12-HSL. Rather than introducing further modifications to the genetic circuit, we focused on controlling experimental conditions that might affect AHL stability and biosensor performance.

Previous research has demonstrated that AHL molecules can undergo pH-dependent lactonolysis, potentially reducing their biological activity.[4] Therefore, we paid particular attention to pH control and minimized unnecessary light exposure during AHL handling and preparation.

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 3 and stage == 'test' %}

To evaluate the fluorescence response of our LasR-based biosensor, we performed a time-course assay using synthetic 3-oxo-C12-HSL. Both the ProD- and J23101-based constructs were tested, and fluorescence was monitored using a 96-well plate reader. (Fig. D11)

Both constructs exhibited increasing fluorescence over time, with the J23101-based construct showing higher fluorescence intensity than the ProD-based construct under the tested conditions. Statistical analysis revealed significantly higher fluorescence in both engineered strains compared with the negative control, which carried the empty vector pBBR1MCS-1 — the same resistance marker, but no sfGFP (Dunnett’s test, p < 0.001). (Fig. D12)

However, our experimental design has an important limitation. Our negative control carried the empty vector pBBR1MCS-1, which has the resistance marker but no sfGFP gene, rather than the corresponding biosensor strain without AHL induction. Consequently, although the observed differences confirmed higher fluorescence in the engineered strains, they could not establish whether the fluorescence was specifically induced by AHL or resulted from basal reporter expression. This is a design flaw we did not notice. Therefore, while measurable fluorescence was observed in both genetic constructs, the AHL-dependent response of our biosensor remained unverified.

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 3 and stage == 'learn' %}

Throughout our engineering cycles, we realized that biosensor performance depends not only on genetic design but also on experimental conditions.

Initially, we suspected that modifications to the promoter and LasR coding sequence contributed to our unsuccessful fluorescence detection. However, reconstructing the reference design did not improve the results, leading us to investigate AHL stability, culture conditions, and experimental procedures.

In our final cycle, we repeated the liquid-culture assay under more carefully controlled conditions. Both constructs exhibited increasing fluorescence over time, with statistically significant differences compared with the negative control. However, we later recognized a critical flaw in our control design: the negative control lacked the sfGFP-containing plasmid. Consequently, we could not distinguish AHL-induced fluorescence from basal reporter expression.

This experience taught us that statistical significance does not necessarily confirm biological functionality. Appropriate controls are essential for interpreting experimental results and validating biological mechanisms.

Although we were unable to conclusively demonstrate AHL-dependent induction, each engineering cycle deepened our understanding of synthetic biology. We learned to question our assumptions, systematically investigate unexpected results, and recognize the importance of experimental design. These lessons will guide future improvements to our biosensor.

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'design' %}

To develop a system capable of detecting Pseudomonas aeruginosa, we designed a biosensor that responds to its quorum-sensing signals. We selected the LasR regulatory system, one of the major QS systems in P. aeruginosa, as the foundation of our biosensor.

LasR protein is able to bind the AHL, 3-oxo-C12-HSL, forming a dimer. Upon binding to the AHL, this active dimer can activate the LasR-responsive promoter, pLasRV, and consequently drive the transcription of the downstream sfGFP gene.[1]

To further enhance the practical applicability of our biosensor, we designed it for integration with our hardware platform, enabling the fluorescence output to be detected and analyzed.

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'build' %}

Following our design, we constructed a plasmid based on the original LasRV biosensor described by Wen et al.[1] In our initial construct, we decided to replace J23101 with ProD, a strong insulated constitutive promoter characterized by Davis et al.,[2] expecting to get better LasR expression. (Fig. D1)

The redesigned genetic circuit retained the LasR-responsive promoter pLasRV and the downstream sfGFP reporter gene. Thus, our detection system 1.0 was born, carried on a pTwist backbone with chloramphenicol resistance and a high-copy ori gene. The new plasmid was subsequently introduced into E. coli TOP10 for further fluorescence testing. (Fig. D2)

Through this construction, we aimed to create a bacterial platform capable of converting AHL signals into detectable fluorescence, allowing us to evaluate the functionality of our biosensing system in subsequent experiments. Our goal is to combine this with the hardware designed by our dry lab team members.

A correct plasmid does not guarantee that the protein is made, so we checked for LasR itself. Whole-cell lysates of the engineered E. coli were separated by SDS-PAGE alongside a lysate of wild-type P. aeruginosa PAO1 for comparison. The engineered strain showed a band at about 26 kDa, the predicted mass of LasR, while no comparable band was visible in the PAO1 lysate. The construct therefore does express the receptor our biosensor depends on. (Fig. D3)

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'test' %}

To evaluate the performance of our biosensor, we initially ordered chemically synthesized 3-oxo-C12-HSL from Sigma. Overnight cultures of engineered E. coli were diluted to an OD600 of 0.3, followed by the addition of AHL. Fluorescence was then monitored over time. However, no detectable fluorescence was observed.

Following this unsuccessful attempt, we explored an alternative approach by co-culturing our engineered E. coli with Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, a natural producer of quorum-sensing signals. Although a visible green coloration appeared in the culture (Fig. D4), no detectable fluorescence was observed, indicating that the color change could not be confidently attributed to sfGFP expression.

We then switched to an agar diffusion assay.[3] Engineered E. coli was mixed with soft agar and overlaid onto an LB agar plate. A well was created in the center of the plate, and PAO1 culture supernatant was introduced into it. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter before being applied to the agar well. This method cannot use synthetic AHL, because the AHL requires DMSO to dissolve. However, excessive DMSO will lead to bacterial death. Under these conditions, observable fluorescence was successfully detected. (Fig. D5) To check that the halo came from our construct rather than from the supernatant itself, we ran the same assay with plasmid-free E. coli TOP10; no halo appeared. (Fig. D6) However, function of fluorescence differed from our initial expectations, suggesting that the biosensor’s response was not yet fully understood.

{% elif part == 'detect' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'learn' %}

As this was our first experience in synthetic biology, we initially underestimated the complexity of engineering biological systems. We construct the plasmid, and subsequently apply AHL molecules to it. Then we expected it will show the result we want.

Although fluorescence was observed in the agar diffusion assay using PAO1 supernatant, the lack of detectable fluorescence in other experimental conditions raised concerns about the performance of our genetic circuit.

In the refered design,[1] LasR expression was regulated by the constitutive promoter BBa_J23101. However, we had replaced J23101 with ProD, an insulated promoter previously reported to exhibit higher expression activity than J23101 under the conditions examined by Davis et al.[2] This modification was intended to enhance LasR expression and potentially improve the sensitivity of our biosensor. Nevertheless, we realized that stronger promoter activity does not necessarily guarantee better biosensor performance, as excessive expression may introduce additional metabolic burdens or affect the balance of the regulatory system.

In addition, we performed codon optimization on the LasR coding sequence to improve its expression in E. coli. However, sequence blast revealed an unexpected amino acid substitution relative to the reference LasR protein. We hypothesized that this substitution might have affected its structure or regulatory activity.

Unideal experiment result raised our concerns about the reliability and consistency of the biosensor’s response. After further discussion, we decided to construct a new plasmid, carefully reviewing the genetic circuit to identify potential design issues.

{% elif part == 'inhibition' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'design' %}

Aptamer is a short sequence of nucleic acid, which is able to bind with the AHL of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. We found a group of ssDNA aptamers, ALS-5, A-16-2 and Cb-2, which are able to bind with the AHL molecules of Pseudomonas aeruginosa——3-oxo-C12-HSL and C4-HSL.[6] While the AHL molecules are captured, biofilm formation and motility would be inhibited. Besides, we also found a system called retron, which is able to generate ssDNA inside E. coli. Retrons occur naturally in bacteria as anti-phage defence systems, where the reverse-transcribed DNA is part of an abortive-infection response.[9] Currently, constructing aptamer by chemical synthesis is mainstream. However, they must be produced externally and supplied to the system as a pre-synthesized reagent. For applications requiring continuous aptamer availability, repeated supplementation may therefore be necessary. We chose Cb-2 aptamer to experiment, for its ability to bind two kinds of AHL simultaneously, while the other aptamers can only bind with one. We aimed to establish a biological production system that could generate the aptamer directly inside living cells. This strategy could reduce the dependence on repeated external addition of synthetic aptamers and provide a programmable platform for intracellular ssDNA production.

In order to synthesize the ssDNA, we utilize the system called retron. This system allows E. coli to generate the single-stranded DNA in vivo. The retron system mainly consists of three parts ——two regions of non-coding RNA msd and msr, and a reverse transcriptase (RT), which is able to reverse transcribe the RNA sequence of the msd to ssDNA. The msr is a conserved sequence, which forms stem structure for the reverse transcriptase to bind to the RNA. In addition, the msd is the sequence that the RT will reverse transcribe.[7]

{% elif part == 'inhibition' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'build' %}

We constructed the engineered retron system in a pTwist vector, in which the Cb-2 aptamer sequence was incorporated into the msd region of the retron. The Ec67 retron system is driven by T7 promoter, consisting of msr, the engineered msd region carrying the Cb-2 sequence, and the Ec67 reverse transcriptase (RT) coding sequence.

The purpose of this build was to create a bacterial platform capable of producing Cb-2-containing ssDNA intracellularly rather than supplying synthetic aptamer externally. First, we transformed the plasmid into E. coli TOP10 strain for propagation. Subsequently, we extracted the plasmid from TOP10, and transformed it into BL21(DE3). The transformed colonies were subsequently verified by colony PCR before the confirmed construct was used for further characterization.

{% elif part == 'inhibition' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'test' %} {# Two tests; the functional one has three assays under it. Each body is a placeholder until the wet lab results are confirmed. #}

1Cb-2 Production

In order to put the Cb-2 aptamer into function, we used a TRIzol-based extraction method to extract the intracellular nucleic acids.

At first, we intended to use gel electrophoresis to determine if the aptamer is indeed produced. The paper we referenced used urea-PAGE and SYBR-Gold. Nonetheless, our laboratory was not equipped to perform denaturing urea-PAGE during the project period; thus we initially used 2% agarose gel to analyze.

First, we cultured 2 ml of E. coli to extract, and it showed nothing on the gel after our first TRIzol extraction. We hypothesized that the production rate of the aptamer was too low to show on the gel. Consequently, we decided to culture 100 ml of the bacteria each time. After this modification, a low-molecular-weight signal became visible on the gel.

As Cb-2 is 93 nts, we initially interpreted this low-molecular-weight signal as a possible product of the Cb-2, and thus we subsequently designed the functional test. Since the Cb-2 aptamer was designed to interfere with quorum-sensing signaling, we selected three quorum-sensing-associated phenotypes for functional evaluation: swarming motility, pyocyanin production and biofilm inhibition, each of which is explained below.

However, we later thought the evidence from the agarose gel was not strong enough, as a result, we performed a further experiment. We compared the result with a BL21(DE3) strain without the plasmid. The result cast doubt on our initial interpretation.

The negative-control BL21(DE3) sample also showed a low-molecular-weight band. And after we applied RNase to the sample, the band disappeared. The disappearance of the band after RNase treatment suggested that RNA contributed substantially to the signal observed in our initial gel. Therefore, the band could not be regarded as direct evidence of retron-derived Cb-2 ssDNA production.

We realized that the evidence obtained from agarose gel electrophoresis was insufficient to confirm successful aptamer production. The conventional agarose gel electrophoresis might not be suitable for detecting and identifying short ssDNA molecules under our experimental conditions. Due to their small size and potential secondary structures, ssDNA is difficult to distinguish from other nucleic acids.

A denaturing urea-PAGE system would provide better resolution for short nucleic acids and reduce the influence of secondary structures by disrupting hydrogen bonds.[6] However, since this method was not available in our laboratory during the project period, further molecular verification of the Cb-2 ssDNA remains necessary.

2Functional test

In the functional assays, we compared three aptamers reported in the reference study: ALS-5, A-16-2, and Cb-2. ALS-5 and A-16-2 were chemically synthesized by IDT and used as reference aptamers, while the Cb-2 group was derived from our engineered retron-Cb-2 system.

Because the Cb-2 group was supplied as a crude TRIzol extract rather than a purified oligonucleotide, we added a fourth group as an extract control. It is an extract of plain E. coli BL21(DE3) — the same expression strain that hosts our retron construct, but untransformed, carrying no retron plasmid at all. The cells were grown to the same density in the same medium, the same culture volume was harvested, and the nucleic acid was extracted by the same TRIzol procedure, so the only difference between this preparation and the Cb-2 preparation is the retron plasmid and whatever it makes.

That is what the group is for. Anything the extraction brings with it rather than the aptamer — residual RNA, carried-over solvent, medium components, the TRIzol chemistry itself — is in this extract just as much as in the Cb-2 extract. If one of those were what suppressed the phenotype, this group would suppress it too. It was run alongside the other groups in all three assays, and is the rightmost bar, labelled BL21, in each of the charts below.

1Swarming

Initially, swarming plates were prepared according to the formulation described in the original study, while the solidification and drying intervals followed the procedure used during our laboratory training. After autoclaving, the medium was allowed to solidify for 40 min, and the poured plates were air-dried for a further 30 min before inoculation. Plates were then incubated for 24 h. Under these conditions, the bacteria failed to spread across the agar surface and instead remained confined to compact, clustered colonies at the inoculation site (Fig. I8), in contrast to the extensive dendritic spreading reported in the original study.

We hypothesized that two factors contributed to this outcome. One was excessive evaporation during drying, which had left the surface too dry to support the hydrated interfacial film required for swarming migration. The other was insufficient ambient humidity during incubation, which would similarly accelerate surface desiccation over the 24 h culture period.

These parameters were then adjusted iteratively. Extending the solidification time to 2 h, shortening the air-drying step to 10 min, and enclosing the plates in a sealed plastic bag to limit evaporative loss during incubation together restored the dendritic spreading pattern described in the original study (Fig. I9). Under these optimized conditions, the swarming radius was measured in ImageJ and compared between Cb-2–treated and control plates. The same plates were also measured with LasReader’s Plate Imaging · Agar Vision, built by our Dry Lab team, as a cross-check on the manual ImageJ measurement. A one-sample t test of the normalized values against the control confirmed a significant reduction in swarming radius. Cb-2 treatment reduced the swarming radius to 80.71 ± 5.52% of the control (n = 5), corresponding to an approximately 19.3% reduction (p < 0.0001). The extract control — the same TRIzol preparation made from untransformed BL21(DE3), carrying no retron plasmid — measured 102.3 ± 0.049% of the untreated control (n = 5) and did not differ significantly from it, so the reduction cannot be attributed to the extract itself. Taken together, this indicates that Cb-2 suppresses population-level surface motility. (Fig. I10)

2Pyocyanin

Pyocyanin is a virulence-associated metabolite produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa. To quantify pyocyanin production, chloroform was added to the bacterial supernatant for extraction. When pyocyanin partitions into the chloroform, the organic phase should turn blue.

Then, the chloroform extract was mixed with 0.2 M HCl, which would make the aqueous phase turn pink. We subsequently measured the absorbance at 520 nm using a 96-well plate reader. The result of the first time we conducted the experiment didn’t go well. The statistics showed no evident inhibition. We examined our experimental procedures: the chloroform should turn blue after combining with the pyocyanin molecules. However, we didn’t see this change at first. (Fig. I11)

We attributed this result to insufficient mixing of the chloroform and the sample. After refining our experiment method, we observed the color change of pyocyanin partitioning into the chloroform (Fig. I12) and the aqueous phase turning pink when added with HCl. (Fig. I13) Reduction of pyocyanin is also observed. Compared with the untreated control, normalized pyocyanin production decreased to 73.72 ± 6.14% in the Cb-2 group (n = 4), corresponding to an inhibition rate of 26.3%. The extract control from retron-free BL21(DE3) measured 98.6 ± 0.77% (n = 3) and was not significantly different from the untreated control. (Fig. I14) Detailed statistical analyses are presented on the Results page.

3Biofilm

Biofilm formation was quantified by crystal violet staining. Overnight cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1, applied with aptamers and dispensed into 96-well plates and incubated statically for 48 hours.

After incubation, the planktonic supernatant was carefully aspirated, and the wells were washed twice with Milli-Q water to remove residual non-adherent cells. Adherent biofilm was stained with crystal violet, and the retained dye was solubilized in acetic acid (Fig. I15) before absorbance was recorded at 550 nm. Absorbance values were corrected by subtracting the mean of sterile medium-only wells processed in parallel, and the corrected values were normalized to the LB-treated control, set at 100%.

Cb-2 treatment reduced biofilm formation to 65.76 ± 10.93% of the control (n = 6), corresponding to an inhibition of approximately 34% (Fig. I16), which suggests that Cb-2 suppresses biofilm development in addition to the population-level motility phenotype described above. The extract control from retron-free BL21(DE3) went the other way, to 106.9 ± 7.2% of the untreated control (n = 6), and was not significantly different from it.

{% elif part == 'inhibition' and cycle == 1 and stage == 'learn' %}

Through these experiments, we learned that functional evidence and molecular verification should be interpreted separately. Although our agarose gel results were insufficient to directly confirm retron-derived Cb-2 ssDNA production, the swarming, pyocyanin, and biofilm assays all showed inhibitory effects consistent with the expected function of Cb-2.

The agreement across three different phenotypic assays provides meaningful functional support for our retron-Cb-2 system and makes further molecular verification worthwhile. We therefore see molecular confirmation not as a missing endpoint, but as the key next step for strengthening the evidence behind the system.

Running an extract control was what let us say that much. An extract prepared exactly the same way from untransformed BL21(DE3) — the same strain, grown and harvested the same way, differing only in that it carries no retron plasmid — changed none of the three phenotypes, which rules out the extraction matrix as the source of the effect. It does not go the whole way: it shows that something the retron plasmid adds to the extract is active, not that the active species is the Cb-2 ssDNA itself. That is the gap urea-PAGE would close.

A further limitation is that the three treatments were not dose-matched. A16-2 and ALS-5 were chemically synthesized at known concentrations, whereas the Cb-2 group was derived from a crude TRIzol extract with an unknown aptamer concentration. Therefore, differences in effect size should not be interpreted as direct differences in aptamer potency. Despite this limitation, the retron-Cb-2 group consistently showed inhibitory effects across all three functional assays.

In future work, we would focus on directly verifying and quantifying retron-derived ssDNA using methods such as denaturing urea-PAGE or sequence-specific detection. This would allow us to better connect aptamer production with the functional effects observed in our assays.

Ultimately, we hope to develop the retron system into a more reliable biological platform for continuous production of functional DNA aptamers in vivo.

A fluorescence reader normally tells green light from blue with an emission filter. We set out to do without one: a single 470 nm LED lights the sample, a ten-channel spectral sensor looks at it from 90°, where the beam does not go, and a light trap behind the cuvette swallows the beam that passes straight through. What the sensor sees is then a matter of which of its channels respond, not of what a filter let past.

The reasoning behind that geometry, and the optics it depends on, are set out under how the reader works.

The parts, the wiring and what it cost are listed under how to build it.

It has voided cycles since; the reader does not claim never to leak, it claims to notice.

Across three mixing sessions, all 27 readings of tubes without GFP came back zero, and 35 of the 38 tubes with GFP read above zero. The cells in those tubes were a test strain — E. coli TOP10 carrying a plasmid that makes sfGFP on its own — not the biosensor, so this says the reader can see the reporter, not that it can see the signal.

Neither is a fault in the optics, and that is what made the second round about the fittings rather than the physics. The feedback is under what using it was like.

After confirming the function of our retron system, we aim to integrate our retron-Cb-2 system with an AHL-responsive lysis module, enabling engineered E. coli to detect quorum-sensing signals from Pseudomonas aeruginosa and subsequently release the intracellularly produced aptamer. We have already attempted to construct such a system, changing the sfGFP region to E7 lysis protein (Fig. I17); however, the lysis module has not yet been successfully validated. For future clinical applications, the safety of bacterial lysis would also need to be carefully considered. Lysis of Gram-negative bacteria may release cellular debris and inflammatory components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which could trigger unwanted immune responses. Therefore, future development should focus not only on improving lysis efficiency, but also on safer release strategies, such as controlled secretion or alternative chassis with lower inflammatory risk.

Overall Learn

Looking back on our iGEM journey, we realized how much we had grown throughout this project. At the beginning, most of us had little experience in synthetic biology. We initially believed that designing a genetic circuit and assembling the necessary components would be enough to achieve our expected results. However, as we began our experiments, we soon realized that biological systems were far more complex than we had imagined.

Throughout our engineering cycles, we gradually learned how to design genetic circuits, plan experiments, troubleshoot unexpected results, and critically analyze our data. From reconsidering our genetic designs to investigating experimental conditions and recognizing the importance of appropriate controls, each challenge encouraged us to question our assumptions and think more carefully about our next steps.

Our Detection and Inhibition systems presented different challenges and taught us different lessons. Although our final results were not as ideal as we had hoped, both projects helped us understand that biological engineering involves much more than simply putting genetic parts together. Even small changes in genetic design or experimental conditions can lead to unexpected outcomes, making biological systems both challenging and fascinating to study.

More importantly, we learned to appreciate the beauty and complexity of synthetic biology. What initially seemed like a straightforward engineering process gradually became an exploration of how biological components interact and how unexpected results can lead to new questions and discoveries.

From knowing almost nothing about synthetic biology to developing our own designs and learning from our experiments, this journey has changed the way we approach scientific problems. Ultimately, we learned that the value of engineering lies not only in achieving the desired outcome, but also in the knowledge, experience, and curiosity we gain along the way.
