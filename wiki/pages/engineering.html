Engineering Success

We are building a cell-free biosensor that detects the bacteria behind ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Our work follows the engineering design cycle; select a piece to read that stage.

Select a piece to open that stage.

Stage 1

Design

The problem we are designing for

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is one of the common hospital-acquired infections in intensive care units, and usually appears in patients who have been on a ventilator for more than 48 hours. It raises mortality, lengthens hospital stays, and places a burden on medical resources.

Diagnosis currently relies on sputum culture and imaging, which leaves three gaps we set out to close:

  • Culture is slow, taking roughly 24–72 hours.
  • It cannot reflect the state of the infection in real time.
  • Antibiotic treatment can produce false negatives.

Why we chose a cell-free system

A cell-free system takes the transcription and translation machinery out of the cell — ribosomes, enzymes and the rest — and expresses protein in vitro. Compared with a living-cell system, that gives us four properties we need for a bedside test:

  • The reaction is fast, giving a result within hours.
  • Nothing has to be kept alive, so handling is simpler.
  • It can be made into a test strip or a portable point-of-care device.
  • It is safer: no live engineered bacteria are released.

What we are detecting

We selected two bacteria commonly associated with VAP:

  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  • Staphylococcus aureus

and three candidate classes of signal to detect them by:

  • quorum sensing molecules
  • specific RNA sequences
  • bacterial secreted metabolites

The gene circuit

The circuit is designed to run inside the cell-free system in three parts:

  1. Input — a bacterial signal molecule
  2. Sensor module — the sensing element, a transcription factor or a riboswitch
  3. Output — a reporter protein: GFP, or a colorimetric enzyme

What we need this design to achieve

  • A cell-free biosensor that recognises signals from VAP bacteria.
  • A gene circuit with high specificity.
  • A visible output — a colour change or a fluorescent signal.
  • Sensitivity and response time we can measure.
  • A prototype with a route to clinical use.

Build

How we will build it

  1. Prepare the transcription–translation system from E. coli lysate.
  2. Add the DNA template carrying the circuit we designed.
  3. Run the reaction against a range of signal-molecule concentrations.

What we will measure

  • Response time.
