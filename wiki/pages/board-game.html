Behind the game

How The Green Cabinet was made

  1. Board Game Introduction

    The Green Cabinet is an educational board game centred on the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), combining strategy, cooperative-competitive dynamics, resource management, and event-card mechanics. Players take on the role of leaders from different political factions, each advancing 3 of their own assigned SDG targets. By playing action cards, triggering events, and using special action cards, players compete and collaborate within a shared “international council” setting, racing to be the first to bring their sustainability targets to the finish line (GOAL).

    The core concept the game aims to convey is that sustainable development has no single path forward — every policy can bring both positive and negative effects at once, and players must constantly make trade-offs between resource allocation and political manoeuvring.

  2. Sources of Inspiration

    The motivation behind this game came from a desire to help people genuinely understand the substance of the SDGs, rather than simply memorising the names of the seventeen goals. We wanted players to become naturally familiar with each goal through play, using an edutainment approach, while event cards would expose them to real global issues — helping them learn what different countries have actually been doing to achieve the SDGs, and connecting them to international affairs beyond the game itself.

  3. Theme Development

    We chose the “cabinet” framing because policy, in reality, emerges from exactly this kind of political tug-of-war. By having players act as leaders of different factions within a cabinet, we wanted to let them experience the tension of factional manoeuvring, which adds fun and strategic depth to the game.

    At the same time, we hoped players would come to appreciate that policy decisions are rarely isolated — a single decision often ripples across many different areas at once.

  4. Translating the Rules into Design

    The game’s core loop runs through three phases: Action, Draw-and-Resolve, and Refill. During the Action Phase, a player may play up to 2 action cards, choosing to help themselves, disrupt an opponent, affect all players, or manipulate the relationships between different SDGs. In the Draw-and-Resolve Phase, the top card of the draw pile is immediately flipped and resolved; if it turns out to be a special action card, it is discarded and another card is drawn in its place. In the Refill Phase, players draw back up to a hand of 5 cards.

    The personal progress track is designed so that target cards can move both forward and backward — but once a target reaches GOAL, it locks in place and is no longer affected by anything. This mechanic is meant to echo the idea that “there is no absolutely safe endpoint in sustainable development, but progress already made doesn’t simply vanish.”

    Special action cards (such as Veto, Policy Exemption, and International Sanctions) add a further layer of strategic depth — the game isn’t just a race to the finish line, but also a test of how well players manage resources and timing.

  5. Challenges

    Balancing forward and backward movement

    In early testing, each player tracked only 2 SDG targets, but action cards were spread across all 17 SDG categories. This meant players often couldn’t draw cards relevant to their own targets and were stuck refilling their hand without making progress, which dampened enthusiasm and slowed the overall pace. We addressed this by having the top card of the draw pile flip and resolve immediately at the end of the Action Phase (which is how the Draw-and-Resolve Phase came about), speeding up the game’s pace while also adding more randomness and variation to each round.

    Card design

    Because some SDGs are harder to find real-world events for, those categories ended up with fewer action cards. During design, we did a rough calculation to keep the number of “forward” and “backward” cards roughly balanced, but we haven’t yet statistically verified whether this distribution has a meaningful effect on overall game balance — this remains something to validate further.

    Not being able to capture all the SDGs affected by a single event

    In reality, a single event often affects far more than a handful of SDGs, but due to space constraints on the card, we limited each event card to showing at most 3 affected targets. This representation isn’t fully precise. To address this, when explaining the rules, we specifically encourage players to think beyond what’s printed on the card — to consider whether the event might also be affecting other SDGs not listed.

  6. Playtest Feedback

    We conducted an actual playtest at a workshop on August 2–3, 2026, with high school students (ages 15–18).

    On the qualitative side, players left a number of positive comments, including: “I hope more people get to play this game and learn what the SDGs are,” “I learned about some events that had a real impact on the world,” and “You can tell a lot of care went into making this.”

  7. Directions for Improvement

    Based on feedback from the playtest, we made several corresponding adjustments:

    • Some players noted they “wanted to understand the events better,” so we added event descriptions to the web version, along with Wikipedia links and annotations, so players could gain a deeper understanding of each event and why it affects a particular SDG.
    • Other players suggested ideas like “reversing scores,” “adding images and flip-effect action cards” (with a nod to Uno-style mechanics), “adding a positive/negative flip mechanic (like a parliamentary defection),” and simply “wanting the game to feel more exciting.” In response, we introduced two new special action cards — Stance Reversal (formerly “Step Reversal”) and Back to Square One (formerly “Step Reset”) — to make gameplay more varied and dynamic.

  8. Looking Ahead

    Going forward, we plan to promote both the web and physical versions of the game to elementary, middle, and high school teachers for classroom use, so that students across age groups can better understand the SDGs through play.

    We also hope to bring the online version to Board Game Arena, so more players can experience the game directly on the platform. In addition, we hope to partner with National Cheng Kung University’s on-campus Liwen Bookstore to sell the physical game in-store, or to collaborate with a board game publisher to bring it to market as an official product.
