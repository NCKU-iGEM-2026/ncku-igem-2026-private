Clinical expert

Finding what the clinical setting needs

Dr. Xin Min Liao

Physician, Division of Chest Medicine, National Cheng Kung University Hospital

{{ date_chip('April 10, 2026') }}

Before we committed to a design, we wanted to hear what the clinic actually needs. So we sat down with Dr. Xin Min Liao, a chest physician at National Cheng Kung University Hospital, and asked him how infections are really handled at the bedside.

He began with how antibiotics are actually prescribed. In clinical practice, only about 20% of patients who receive antibiotics are treated on the basis of drug-susceptibility results; the rest are treated empirically, and acutely ill patients in particular depend on the physician’s experience.

We had come with an idea of screening for infection before symptoms appear. Dr. Liao was candid: he did not think that was very feasible, and it would have to be weighed against cost. Instead, he pointed us toward a different need — helping to tell which bacterium is responsible, within a short time. Today’s identification methods take days and are expensive, so a quick way to identify the species would have real potential, even if it could only detect Pseudomonas. He also noted that Haemophilus influenzae is another common cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) in Taiwan.

Then he walked us through where a sample would actually come from. In the ICU, sputum is suctioned about every 2 hours, and about every 4 hours at night; on general wards the frequency varies, and suctioning is usually done by caregivers or family members rather than nurses. A suction machine has a negative-pressure unit at the front, which draws the sputum into a collection canister — and Dr. Liao suggested we design our device to sit at the front end, where the sample would be more accurate.

He left us with encouragement: he believes our approach has great potential in the clinic.

Academic expert

Aptamer? An insightful conversation with Prof. Shiesh

Prof. Shu-Chu Shiesh

Department of Medical Laboratory Science and Biotechnology, National Cheng Kung University

{{ date_chip('May 27, 2026') }}

With the aptamer at the centre of our design, we brought the idea to Prof. Shu-Chu Shiesh of NCKU’s Department of Medical Laboratory Science and Biotechnology.

She told us we would not be the first to point aptamers at infection: others have built aptamer-based detection into a chip integrated with a urinary catheter, also to detect infection. It showed us that the idea of bringing aptamer sensing to the bedside has real precedent.

Then she turned to what makes an aptamer work at all. An aptamer only functions once it has folded into the right structure, and the pH of the buffer affects whether it can fold correctly; it also needs specific ions to do its job. Her suggestion was practical: try buffers in different proportions to find the conditions under which the aptamer works best.

She liked the idea, and encouraged us to give it a try.

In short

{{ flip('There is precedent', 'Aptamer detection has already been made into a chip on a urinary catheter, to detect infection.') }} {{ flip('Structure is everything', 'An aptamer only works once folded correctly — and buffer pH and specific ions decide whether it can.') }} {{ flip('Test the buffer', 'Try buffers in different proportions to find the conditions where the aptamer works best.') }}
Industry · fabrication

Ai Wei Li Laser Engraving Studio: the hardware shell

Ai Wei Li Laser Engraving Studio

{{ date_chip('September 10, 2026') }}

While developing the hardware, thought was put into using cost-effective and reusable materials. Therefore, 3D printing and laser cutting were considered as methods of hardware fabrication. Within the period between July and August, a number of materials and designs were used for the hardware case. Nevertheless, most prototypes could not meet the requirements with respect to strength and efficiency.

In order to improve the design, professional consultation was obtained through working with Ai Wei Li (Laser Engraving Studio). The design files were discussed at the studio, and valuable advice was received concerning material choice and fabrication process. Firstly, it should be pointed out that each substance has its own properties and they should be taken into account during the device construction, including light reflection. The importance of the thickness of the material was stressed, especially at the places where different elements are connected, because the incorrect connection may cause light leakage.

Furthermore, assembly and adjustment of the hardware were performed at the studio under the on-site support of the manufacturer.

In short

{{ flip('Every material behaves differently', 'Account for each one’s properties, light reflection included.') }} {{ flip('Mind the thickness at the joints', 'A poor connection between parts lets light leak in.') }} {{ flip('Build it together', 'The case was assembled and adjusted at the studio, with the maker on hand.') }}

Academic expert

Finding what we need to improve

Prof. Nan Si Li

Department of Biotechnology and Bioindustry Sciences, National Cheng Kung University

{{ date_chip('September 22, 2026') }}

By late September, Capture had a working design, and we wanted someone to look hard for its weak spots. Prof. Nan Si Li of NCKU’s Department of Biotechnology and Bioindustry Sciences did exactly that.

He started from how a project like ours should be framed: with a specific target — which kind of specimen, and whether that specimen contains substances that could degrade our system. A real sample is not a clean one. It may contain antibiotics, it may vary in viscosity, and the body itself contains naturally fluorescent substances — any of which could affect our engineered E. coli.

He was equally careful about what we claim. Looking beyond the competition, he advised us not to say that our system can diagnose P. aeruginosa, but that it can estimate the risk that an infection is caused by P. aeruginosa, as a reference for clinicians.

Finally, the hardware. Its workflow should start from a clear target user. And we could present the technology as one that can later be combined with other hardware: a front-end unit to process the sample, followed by our hardware to run the detection.

In short

{{ flip('Know your specimen', 'Define the target sample, and check what in it could degrade the system or interfere: antibiotics, viscosity, natural fluorescence.') }} {{ flip('Estimate risk, don’t diagnose', 'Present the result as the risk that an infection is caused by P. aeruginosa, as a reference — not a diagnosis.') }} {{ flip('Design the workflow for its users', 'Start from the target user, and frame the hardware as one step: front-end sample processing, then our detection.') }}

Academic expert

Designing for the clinic: priorities, DNA stability and sensor choice

Prof. Tsung Lin Liao

Chair of the Department of Biotechnology and Bioindustry Sciences, National Cheng Kung University

{{ date_chip('September 22, 2026') }}

Prof. Liao began by asking us what we value most in designing and developing our hardware. Weighing the current needs of medical testing, we settled on three priorities: low cost, speed and convenience, with the aim of a testing method that is both accessible and efficient.

Prof. Liao also suggested that we collect and analyze the relevant DNA sequences, so that we can anticipate how enzymes present in patient samples might degrade them. Doing so would help us choose suitable sequences and make our system more stable and reliable for future clinical use.

On the hardware design itself, Prof. Liao encouraged us to explore a wider range of sensors and to make use of their different detection thresholds and characteristics. Experimenting with different sensing mechanisms may reveal new ways to improve the performance of our detection system.

In short

{{ flip('Know your priorities', 'Low cost, speed and convenience, for a test that is accessible and efficient.') }} {{ flip('Plan for the sample', 'Analyze the DNA sequences against the enzymes in patient samples, and choose ones that hold up.') }} {{ flip('Widen the sensors', 'Try sensors with different detection thresholds and characteristics.') }}
