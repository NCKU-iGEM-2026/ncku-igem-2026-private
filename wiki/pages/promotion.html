NCKU Pilot Interdisciplinary Sharing Session

"Sharing our science is not merely about presenting localized results; it is an open invitation to refine our vision through critical dialogue and diverse perspectives."

On May 23, 2026, our team presented at the NCKU Pilot Interdisciplinary Sharing Session, a checkpoint chance to test our early concepts in front of scholars and experts from fields well outside synthetic biology. Because many panelists had never encountered iGEM before, their sharp, unfiltered questions cut straight to assumptions we had stopped questioning ourselves.

That exchange proved pivotal: weighing their feedback, we narrowed our project from a broad entry point around Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia toward a focused investigation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection mechanisms. Admitting the gaps in our original logic was humbling, but it left us with a clearer direction and real momentum going forward.

Lund University Podcast

For collaboration in Human Practices, we really wanted to connect with people beyond our usual circles, so we teamed up with the iGEM team from Lund University in Sweden to record a special Chinese-language podcast episode.

It was such a fun and rewarding experience to sit down and just chat about our project, Capture. We took the opportunity to explain the science behind our fight against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, doing our best to break down complex molecular tools like retrons and aptamers into concepts that anyone tuning in could easily grasp.

But we didn't want the episode to be just a boring science lecture, so we also shared a lot about who we are as people. We opened up about what initially sparked our drive to join iGEM and gave listeners a behind-the-scenes look at our daily lives—sharing the reality of our late-night brainstorming sessions, the inevitable wet lab struggles, and the incredibly tight bond we've built as a team.

Ultimately, working with Lund University was about so much more than just communicating our research; it was a chance to bridge continents, break down language barriers in science, and build a genuine friendship across the global iGEM community.

NCKU UR Interdisciplinary Sharing Session

"Science is a torch passed down across generations—not merely to hand down answers, but to ignite the courage to ask bold, transformative questions."

On September 2, 2026, after two months of summer collaboration across all our sub-teams, we presented at the NCKU UR Interdisciplinary Sharing Session — a chance to pressure-test our project alongside students from fields as far from biology as aerospace and motorsport engineering.

What made this session different was the audience: incoming freshmen just starting their university journeys. Rather than a polished showcase, we walked them through the real arc of the project — how it started, where it stalled, and what we've built since.

We didn't romanticize it. We talked about failed wet-lab trials, late-night pivots, and the self-doubt that comes with them — and why pushing through those moments builds the resilience and problem-solving instincts that matter far beyond the lab. If that honesty gave a few of them a clearer, less intimidating view of what research actually looks like, the session did its job.

Bringing Capture and iGEM to Future Scientists and Doctors

Date: September 30, 2026

On September 30, 2026, the Medical Research Club of National Tainan Girls’ Senior High School invited us to introduce our project to 50 club members. These students were already passionate about medicine and life sciences, yet for many of them, this was their first encounter with synthetic biology and the iGEM competition. That made them exactly the audience we hoped to reach: future scientists and doctors who had not yet seen how engineering biology could connect to the careers they were dreaming of. We built the session around two themes: our 2026 project, Capture, and a question the club itself asked us to answer: “What is medical school actually like?”

Promoting Capture and Synthetic Biology

To introduce Capture from every angle, each subgroup presented its own part of the project: what plasmids are, how we designed and expressed our genes, the core ideas of synthetic biology, and how our hardware went from concept to prototype. Because the students were initially shy about speaking up, we shared an anonymous Google Form so that anyone could ask questions at any time. It collected 31 questions, most of them about the details of our gene circuit. For an audience meeting synthetic biology for the first time, this showed that they were not just hearing about Capture but engaging with its actual design.

Showing That iGEM Is Open to Every Background

In the second half, our team leader Hank, a second-year medical student, explained why he chose medicine, what daily life in medical school looks like, and why a medical student would join a synthetic biology competition. His story showed the students that iGEM is not limited to biology or engineering majors: future doctors can also use synthetic biology to address health problems. This part drew the strongest response of the day, with students asking about the realities of studying medicine and how the health insurance system affects medical practice.

What We Took Away

Instead of running a lab demonstration, we promoted our project through first-hand stories and open conversation. The 31 anonymous questions also showed us that an online question channel helps reach students who would not otherwise speak up. Most importantly, this format allowed students to connect Capture and iGEM with their own interests and future careers, turning a first introduction to synthetic biology into a field they could see themselves in.

Ask Us Anything

A few of the questions club members sent in through the anonymous form — flip a card to see how we answered.

Q

What kind of mental preparation do you need for studying medicine?

A

Basically, you need to know that once you’re in medical school, the pressure will be intense — from coursework and from relationships alike — because in this “ivory tower,” every word and action gets noticed. So you need real stress tolerance, and it helps to have a hobby that lets you unwind.

Q

Which subject do you find the hardest?

A

Immunology, at least for me right now — but honestly, every medical subject demands a huge amount of memorization and study, so it’s fair to say none of them are easy.

Q

As a medical student, what’s your outlook on healthcare over the next ten years?

A

Healthcare has been moving fast — AI in clinical practice, precision medicine, new classes of drugs. That means staying genuinely eager to learn and actively keeping up with it all, so you don’t fall behind the rest of the world.

Q

Do doctors really have to keep attending conferences all the time?

A

Yes! Medical knowledge keeps getting updated, so attending regularly is how you stay current on the latest treatment guidelines and more.

Q

What do you think of Taiwan’s National Health Insurance system?

A

Taiwan’s system is arguably the best in the world, but it’s facing real problems — funding shortages, expensive new drugs, and medical staff pay. The government needs to seriously address the root causes and find ways to raise revenue and cut costs, so it can stay sustainable.

Q

Would medical school be miserable for a humanities-leaning student?

A

Not if you genuinely care about helping people and love medicine — a humanities background can actually help you memorize material faster, and medical humanities matter a great deal too.
