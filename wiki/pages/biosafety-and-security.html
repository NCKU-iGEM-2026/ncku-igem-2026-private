Preface

Almost half a century ago, the geneticist Stanley Cohen and his colleagues told the world that they had built a plasmid — a ring of DNA — carrying a gene from another organism into an E. coli cell. That was the birth of genetic engineering. Since the first genetically modified organisms appeared they have drawn curiosity and inspiration, and a growing unease along with them.

We still take that work, which undoubtedly carries some degree of risk, as a vocation worth pursuing carefully. Winning broad acceptance for synthetic biology in everyday life is part of the same task, and much of that effort is still ahead of us. Biosafety has never mattered more as the science grows: without it, science stays caged in the laboratory, and any medical improvement built on biological products stays a dream that never arrives.

Project overview & safety summary

Our project runs along two routes: it listens for the quorum sensing signals (AHLs) of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and it releases aptamers that interrupt those signals. Detection comes first and acts as navigation — it confirms that the target molecules are really there before we try to quiet the bacteria down.

General laboratory safety rules

Our management rationale follows the Safe Laboratory Practices published by National Cheng Kung University, used here as our reference.[1]

Training & oversight

In accordance with the Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Regulations Governing the Management of Infectious Biological Materials[4] and university policy, a specified number of training hours must be completed before entering a BSL-2 laboratory. That training typically includes:

  • 3 hours of “General Laboratory Safety and Health Training”.
  • 3 hours of “Hazardous Chemical Labeling and General Awareness”, as we use hazardous chemicals.
  • 4 hours of “Biosafety and Biosecurity Training”.
  • Completion of the “Biosafety Knowledge Assessment”.

Only members who have completed the additional BSL-2 training and been authorised by the PI carry out steps involving the pathogen; other members do not. Laboratory notebooks, the strain inventory, labelling and storage locations are managed centrally by our safety officer.

Protective measures

Personal protective equipment, disinfection and the accidental-exposure reporting procedure all follow the current rules of NCKU’s Environmental Protection and Safety Health Center.

  • Personal protective equipment. Lab coat, gloves and safety glasses in both work areas; gloves changed and hands washed on leaving the bench; no food, drink or open shoes in the laboratory.
  • Disinfection. Benches wiped down before and after work; the biological safety cabinet surface-disinfected on the laboratory’s own schedule; a spill is contained, disinfected and reported to the supervisor rather than cleared up alone.
  • Accidental exposure. Stop work, give first aid, tell the supervisor immediately, then report through the NCKU ESH Center’s incident procedure.
  • Access and storage. The laboratory is locked when unattended, strains are stored in a labelled, designated place, and only authorised members hold access.

Waste

  • Biological waste. All plates, media, tips and consumables that have contacted bacteria are collected under NCKU regulations, autoclaved, and then removed through the university’s authorised biomedical waste stream.
  • Chemical waste. Fluorescent dyes, nucleic acid stains and similar reagents are segregated and collected under NCKU’s chemical waste categories.

Shipment

We exchanged samples with other teams this season. We followed iGEM’s policies[2] and the relevant domestic and international rules for biological material, and shipped only plasmid DNA or laboratory E. coli strains — never the pathogen.

The division of labour matters here. All genetic modification happens in E. coli, which is both our detector and our synthetic biology tool; P. aeruginosa is only an organism we test against, and is never genetically modified. No toxin genes and no virulence factors were introduced into our chassis. The only antibiotic-resistance genes present are the standard plasmid selection markers required for cloning — no resistance determinant from a clinical isolate was cloned or transferred.

Four features that make this design safer by construction

  1. The engineered chassis are safe laboratory strains. All cloning and expression is done in laboratory E. coli, never in a pathogen.
  2. We do not enhance anything the bacteria already do. The design goal is to quiet bacterial coordination, rather than to kill and so push virulence or resistance.
  3. We measure molecules, not patients. All experiments this season use purified or synthetic signal molecules, or bacterial culture supernatants, in the laboratory. There are no human samples and no clinical recruitment.
  4. No environmental release. Engineered strains stay inside the laboratory. There is no therapeutic or environmental deployment, and no use on humans or animals.

What we have not done

Our approach through the experiments and the rest of our work is a conservative one, but we expect that the story could be extended, inside synthetic biology or outside it. Those extensions would bring risks of their own — during sample collection, during experiments, or on contact with highly regulated strains.

Our results are an in vitro proof of concept. This is not a diagnostic device and not a drug, and it has not been validated in real clinical specimens such as sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or wound exudate.

The following are not done, and we think it is better to say so plainly for whoever picks the work up next:

  • Biocontainment design. Our current engineered strains carry no kill switch and no auxotrophic dependency. If this ever moves toward real application, that is a necessary next step.
  • Endotoxin, which a kill switch does not solve. Even with a self-destruct design, the host’s own endotoxin (LPS) stays behind in the preparation, so killing the cells does not by itself make an extracted aptamer safe to give to anyone. If this were ever taken toward a drug, removing endotoxin physically or chemically during purification would be the route we would recommend, rather than relying on the kill switch.[6]
  • Moving to a cell-free system. If the reporter reaction can run without cells, the question of live strains escaping disappears entirely. We consider this the cleanest long-term route.
  • Effect on commensal flora. Other bacteria also communicate through AHLs, so interrupting these signals could have unintended ecological effects.[7][8] We have not tested this.
  • Clinical samples and their review. Since our eventual target is a clinical one, collecting and testing samples is an indispensable way to determine whether our system works. If there is ever hospital collaboration or use of clinical isolates, the provenance and the IRB or human-subjects review must be obtained and recorded.
