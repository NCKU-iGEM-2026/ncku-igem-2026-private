  • Green nail

    Pigment secretion & trapped moisture

    When moisture becomes trapped beneath micro-gaps in the nail plate, P. aeruginosa secretes pyocyanin and pyoverdine — a blue-green pigment and a fluorescent one, which diffuse out of the infected tissue into the nail plate and leave a stubborn stain that cannot be washed away.[1][2]

  • Damp sink

    Drain biofilm & aerosol spread

    Fueled by constant moisture and soap residues, bacteria build a gelatinous biofilm inside plumbing drains. Tap water striking the water standing in the trap then throws up aerosols that carry the organism to the hands washed above it, and to the surfaces around.[3][4]

  • Lens case

    Synthetic adhesion & corneal virulence

    P. aeruginosa exhibits an exceptional affinity for synthetic polymers in contact lenses and cases. Once attached, it secretes proteases that digest the cornea — a liquefactive destruction that can run on to perforation within days.[5][6]

  • Swimmer’s ear

    Epithelial maceration & opportunistic attack

    Stagnant water softens and macerates the delicate skin lining the outer ear canal, stripping away protective earwax. P. aeruginosa exploits this breached defense to colonize and trigger acute, painful inflammation.[7][8]

When these four powers — adhesion, protease attack, pigment toxicity, and biofilm formation — combine inside an ICU, a minor everyday nuisance escalates into a life-threatening crisis.

The Global Burden of Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs)

HAIs are infections patients acquire while receiving treatment in healthcare facilities — a growing crisis in modern medicine.

  • How common

    7–15

    in every 100 patients in acute care acquire one — 7 in high-income countries, 15 in low- and middle-income

  • Resistant to antibiotics

    136 Million

    antibiotic-resistant HAIs a year, worldwide

  • In intensive care

    Up to 30%

    of patients in intensive care can be affected

  • 20–40% reported mortality in critically ill patients who go on to severe pneumonia or bacteraemia
  • 26% of P. aeruginosa isolated in Taiwanese medical-centre intensive care is resistant to carbapenems

Two systems, one signal

The two halves of the project read the same molecule and do opposite things with it. System 1 lets the signal through and reports it. System 2 takes the signal out of circulation.

Our project is about Capture: something that has to find its target and hold on to it before the moment passes. What you are catching here is AHL, the signal molecule Pseudomonas aeruginosa releases — not the bacterium itself, because our aptamer is raised against the signal rather than the cell. This is that idea turned into a minute of play, and nothing more — it is here for outreach and for fun, and it is not a model of our system or a representation of any result.
