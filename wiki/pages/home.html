The organism we are after

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is hard to treat because its cells act together

It does its worst in exactly the places medicine cannot avoid creating: ventilators, catheters and open wounds.

  • 32,000 infections a year attributed to it in the United States
  • 2,700 deaths a year, in the same reporting
  • 20–40% reported mortality in critically ill patients who go on to severe pneumonia or bacteraemia
  • 140,000+ healthcare-associated infections a year in Taiwan, of every kind — it is among the top three pathogens behind them

Two things it does. Two things we do.

  1. It coordinates

    Cells release a quorum sensing signal, AHL, and read how much of it is around them. Those messages are there before anything has grown on a culture plate, and they are specific to the organism sending them.

  2. It shields itself

    Enough signal switches on virulence and builds a biofilm that antibiotics struggle to cross. By the time a culture comes back, 48 to 72 hours later, that shield is already up.

  3. We read the message

    Engineered E. coli that fluoresces when it hears AHL, so the infection reports itself rather than waiting on sputum culture.

  4. We take the message away

    An aptamer binds the signal, so the coordination it was carrying never arrives. Taking a signal away is a different kind of pressure from killing, which selects for the survivors that resisted.

Background

Project Background

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is hard to treat not because any single cell is formidable, but because the cells act together — coordinating through quorum sensing chemicals that switch on virulence and build a biofilm antibiotics struggle to cross. Those messages are there before anything has grown on a culture plate, and they are specific to the organism sending them, while diagnosis today still waits 48–72 hours on sputum culture. Capture goes after the messages rather than the bacteria: two systems and one signal, where the first detects that signal and reports the infection, and the second captures it, so the coordination it was carrying never arrives. Taking a signal away is a different kind of pressure from killing, which selects for the survivors that resisted — that is our design rationale, not a result we have measured.

Capture

Our project is about capture: something that has to find its target and hold on to it before the moment passes. What you are catching here is AHL, the signal molecule Pseudomonas aeruginosa releases — not the bacterium itself, because our aptamer is raised against the signal rather than the cell. This is that idea turned into a minute of play, and nothing more — it is here for outreach and for fun, and it is not a model of our system or a representation of any result.
