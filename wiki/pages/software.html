A fluorescent biosensor reports its target as light. Getting from that light to a concentration takes a whole sequence of wet-lab steps: standards, blanks, replicates, a curve, and judgment about where the curve can be trusted.

LasReader turns that sequence into one workflow in the browser, with nothing to install. We built it for our own AHL sensor and our own reader, CAPTURE-Screen, but the analysis serves any team whose biosensor glows.

  • Source code on iGEM GitLab
  • Open the live app
  • MIT License
Research use only. LasReader reports concentrations inferred from a calibration curve. It is not intended for diagnostic use.

Part 1

From bench to number

Each step a wet-lab member does by hand has a matching step in LasReader, done the same way every time and written into the export.

{# 待改寫（說明 B）：這段是一般性的描述，請換成團隊自己原本處理數據的真實經驗。 #}

1.1The problem

A plate reader hands back a grid of numbers. Turning it into concentrations is usually left to whoever holds the data, in a spreadsheet rebuilt for each experiment: subtract the blank, average the replicates, fit a curve, read the samples off it. Each of those steps involves a choice that rarely gets written down, and the result is a single number with no indication of how far to trust it, even when the sample lies beyond the highest standard.

1.2The workflow

Seven steps, in the order they happen at the bench. Choose one to see what LasReader does there.

1Plan the standards

At the bench

Prepare standards of known concentration and blanks with none, next to the samples.

LasReader

  • Asks for at least 4 standard concentrations and 2 blanks, the least a four-parameter curve and a limit of detection need.
  • On CAPTURE-Screen, lays the tubes out in replicate rounds (a blank, then every standard from low to high) so instrument drift falls on every concentration equally.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen → Calibrate

{{ shot('Calibrate: the reading order in replicate rounds', fig='Figure 1a') }}

2Read

At the bench

Read the plate on any plate reader, or read the tubes one by one on CAPTURE-Screen.

LasReader

  • Takes plate readings typed, pasted from Excel, or pasted as the reader's 8 × 12 export and marked on a plate map. Fluorescence can be divided by OD600.
  • On CAPTURE-Screen, each tube is one click: a dark → light → dark read of about 3 s, with dark subtraction and normalization done for you.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen

{{ shot('Plate Reader Assay: the plate map', fig='Figure 1b') }}

3Check the data

At the bench

Make sure the instrument behaves and that no well or tube is off.

LasReader

  • Grades CAPTURE-Screen with a buffer-only self-check: dark stability and saturation.
  • Flags each tube that saturated, missed a dark frame, scatters far more than the blank, or was read under different settings.
  • Lets you exclude a reading only with a written reason. It stays on the plot and in the export.

In CAPTURE-Screen → Instrument, and every fit

{{ shot('Excluding a reading, with its reason', fig='Figure 1c') }}

4Fit the curve

At the bench

Draw a standard curve through the standards.

LasReader

  • Fits a weighted four-parameter logistic curve (§2.1, Eqs. 1–2).
  • Works out the limit of detection and quantification from the blanks, and the usable range: the only stretch of the curve that returns a number (§2.1, Eq. 3).

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen

{{ shot('A fitted curve with its LOD and usable range', fig='Figure 1d') }}

5Convert the samples

At the bench

Read each sample's concentration off the curve.

LasReader

  • Averages a sample's replicates first, then converts once, and scales by its dilution.
  • Gives every concentration a 95% confidence interval (§2.1, Eqs. 4–5).
  • Outside the usable range, says "below LOD" or "above range" and gives no number.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen → Measure

{{ shot('Sample results with their 95% CIs', fig='Figure 1e') }}

6Compare instruments

At the bench

Measure the same samples on a reference plate reader and on your own reader.

LasReader

  • Pairs the two results by sample name.
  • Shows a log-log plot against y = x, the geometric mean ratio, Bland–Altman 95% limits of agreement, and how many confidence intervals overlap (§2.1, Eq. 6).

In Cross-Validation

{{ shot('Cross-Validation: log-log plot and agreement', fig='Figure 1f') }}

7Export and reproduce

At the bench

Record the results so that someone else can check them.

LasReader

  • Exports a CSV that opens in Excel, with every reading, every exclusion and its reason, and every result. The curve comes as a PNG.
  • Backs up all CAPTURE-Screen runs, curves and batches to one JSON file.
  • Lets anyone refit a curve from its file and get the same numbers.

1.3Three tools, one workflow

The workflow runs on three tools. Two take readings from different instruments through the same curve; the third compares them.

Any plate reader

Plate Reader Assay

Standards and samples read together on any plate reader become a standard curve, with each sample's concentration and its 95% CI. Steps 1–5, 7

  1. Enter data. One row per standard (0 for blanks) or sample, 1–6 replicates. Type, paste from Excel, or paste the 8 × 12 plate and mark the wells.
  2. Fit. Exclude a reading only with a reason, then fit the curve.
  3. Results. Each sample in the well and in the original sample, with a 95% CI.
  4. Export. CSV and PNG. Nothing is stored or uploaded.
{{ shot('Plate Reader Assay: results', fig='Figure 2') }}
Our instrument

CAPTURE-Screen

The control software for our low-cost fluorescence reader (ESP32 + AS7341). A step bar on every page shows what comes next. Steps 1–5, 7

  1. Instrument. Connection status and a buffer-only self-check.
  2. Calibrate. Read each tube in the order shown, or type in recorded readings, then fit and save the curve.
  3. Curves. Each saved curve, its limits, and whether it still matches the instrument's settings.
  4. Measure. Choose a curve, read blanks, then samples in replicate, and export.

The landing page shows all ten spectral channels live while the device is on. The instrument itself is described on the Hardware page.

{{ shot('CAPTURE-Screen: live spectrum', fig='Figure 3') }}
Validation

Cross-Validation

The same samples measured on a plate reader and on CAPTURE-Screen, paired by name: the check that a low-cost reader agrees with a commercial one. Step 6

  1. Plate reader. Load the Plate Reader Assay's CSV.
  2. CAPTURE-Screen. Choose a measurement batch, or load its CSV, with its dilution.
  3. Compare. Table, log-log plot, geometric mean ratio and limits of agreement.
  4. Export. CSV and PNG.

Part 2

How it decides

Five ideas carry the whole model. The full derivation, sixteen sections of it, is in the repository's docs/capture_screen_model.md.

2.1Five key ideas

1A curve shaped like the sensor

A biosensor saturates, so its response is S-shaped on a log concentration axis. LasReader fits the four-parameter logistic, whose four numbers each mean something at the bench: the signal with no target B, the saturated signal T, the half-response concentration EC50, and the steepness h. Blanks enter at c = 0, where the curve equals B, so they pin the baseline directly.

F(c) = B + {{ frac('T − B', '1 + (EC50 / c)h') }} (1)

2Noisy readings count for less

Replicates scatter more at high signal than at low, so an ordinary fit would let the top standards dominate. LasReader estimates how the spread grows from the replicates themselves, an additive part a and a proportional part b, and weights each reading by the inverse of its variance in a Levenberg–Marquardt fit.

Var(F) = a + bF2, wi ≈ {{ frac('1', 'a + byi2') }} (2)

3Know where the curve can be trusted

The limit of detection is the blank level plus three blank standard deviations, taken through the curve; the limit of quantification uses ten. A number is returned only between the larger of the LOD and the lowest standard, and the smaller of the highest standard and the point where the curve reaches 95% of its span. Beyond that the curve is so flat that a tiny change in signal means a huge change in concentration. LasReader never extrapolates.

cmin = max(LOD, lowest standard), cmax = min(highest standard, EC50·191/h) (3)

4Every number comes with an interval

A sample's signal is turned back into a concentration through the inverse curve. Its 95% confidence interval combines two sources: the noise of the reading, which shrinks with more replicates n, and the uncertainty of the curve itself, which does not. The interval is worked out on ln c (the delta method), because concentration errors are multiplicative.

ln c = ln EC50 + {{ frac('1', 'h') }} ln {{ frac('F − B', 'T − F') }} (4)
Var(ln c) ≈ gF2 {{ frac('a + bF2', 'n') }} + gθ⊤ Σθ gθ (5)

5One piece of code for both instruments

The Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen call the same fitting module, curve_fit.js, so the same readings give the same curve on either. When Cross-Validation finds a difference, it comes from the instruments, not from two pieces of arithmetic. Agreement is measured on the log scale, with di the log ratio of the two results for sample i:

GMR = 10d, LoA95 = [ 10d − 1.96sd, 10d + 1.96sd ] (6)

Ideas 1–4 can be tried on your own readings in §3.2, which runs the same code.

2.2Rules it keeps

Table 1. Rules the software keeps
RuleHow it is enforced
No concentration without a valid conversionA number is shown only inside the usable range; otherwise only the bound.
No simulated device, no demo dataWhen CAPTURE-Screen is off, the pages say it is offline and never fall back to made-up readings.
A curve only converts what it was made forA CAPTURE-Screen curve converts only readings taken with the same instrument settings and signal; the biosensor strain is recorded with the curve and every batch.
A saved curve never changesRefitting with different exclusions creates a new curve, so what a batch converted stays reproducible.
Nothing disappears silentlyExcluded readings stay in the run, on the plot and in the export, with the reason given.

Part 3

Yours to use

Nothing in the analysis is specific to our sensor. A team with a different fluorescent biosensor can use it today, and most changes beyond that are one file.

3.1Who can use it

LasReader was built for our AHL biosensor, so its pages say "AHL" and label concentrations in nM. The analysis never uses either: it fits standards of known concentration to their readings and converts samples through that curve. Any unit works, as long as standards and samples share it.

3.3Run it, build on it

To use LasReader you need only a browser: open the live app. To run it yourself you need Python 3.14 and Git; the frontend has no build step and needs no Node.js. 

git clone https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/ncku-tainan/lasreader.git
cd lasreader
bash scripts/setup.sh      # once
bash scripts/dev.sh        # backend on :8000, frontend on :5500

On Windows PowerShell, run scripts\setup.ps1 and scripts\dev.ps1 instead. cd backend; pytest runs the 45 backend tests, and the API documents itself at /docs.

Also in LasReader

Swarming Assay

A separate tool for a phenotype: how far a swarming colony has spread, measured from a photo of its plate.

Input
Plate photos taken from above, up to 24 per batch, each with its strain, condition and replicate.
Scale
The plate is found in the photo, and its known diameter (93 mm by default, adjustable) turns pixels into millimetres.
Detection
The background is corrected, colonies are thresholded, and a region that looks like more than one colony is split. Photos on a purple UV background are detected and handled separately.
Output
Each colony's longest span, equivalent diameter and area, in one CSV for the batch, and every photo returned annotated so you can check the plate edge was found.
Reproducible
The analysis is deterministic: the same photos and plate diameter give the same numbers. The CSV records both on every row.

The analysis is Tzu-Chiao Chou's colony-measurement code, run unchanged on the backend in Python with OpenCV. The backend keeps neither the photos nor the results.
