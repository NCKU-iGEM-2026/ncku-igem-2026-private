A fluorescent biosensor reports its target as light, but a team needs a concentration. In between sit standards, blanks, replicates, a curve, and a decision about which part of that curve can be used, usually worked out by hand in a spreadsheet.

LasReader turns that whole process, from reading fluorescence to an AHL concentration, into one clear workflow in the browser, with nothing to install. With CAPTURE-Screen, our low-cost reader (Hardware), the instrument is part of the workflow: LasReader checks the reader, shows which tube to read next, reads each one in a click, and gives every sample's concentration with an approximate 95% confidence interval. Data from any plate reader goes through the same steps.

We built it for our AHL sensor (Description), but the analysis never uses the target's name. It can be used with other biosensors whose rising response is suitable for a 4PL calibration, provided the fit is checked against their experimental data.

Part 1

One workflow, bench to concentration

Seven steps take a sample from the bench to a concentration. For each one, this part shows what you do and what LasReader and CAPTURE-Screen do for you.

1.1What it solves

Without LasReader With LasReader

Without LasReader Hard to repeat. Each person's spreadsheet makes different choices, and few of them are recorded.

With LasReader Same steps, every time. Each one is recorded in the export.

Without LasReader Slow to a result. Fitting a curve and reading every sample off it takes time, and is easy to get wrong.

With LasReader A curve in seconds. Paste the readings and press Fit: the curve and every sample's concentration, with its approximate 95% confidence interval, come back at once.

Without LasReader One tool per instrument. Each reader has its own software and format, and a home-built one often has none.

With LasReader One tool for both. It runs our CAPTURE-Screen directly and takes data from any commercial plate reader, through the same curve.

Prof. Nan-Si Li advised us to design the whole workflow, sample to answer: preparing the specimen, the sensing reaction, reading the fluorescence on a portable instrument, and interpreting the data in software (Meet with experts). CAPTURE-Screen is the third of those steps and LasReader the fourth.

1.3The workflow

Seven steps, in the order they happen at the bench. Choose one to see what you do and what LasReader does.

The blanks have different roles; prepare the one each step requires:

Medium blank
Medium without cells. Its mean fluorescence and OD600 are subtracted before the Plate Reader Assay divides fluorescence by OD600.
Cell blank
Biosensor cells without AHL, prepared under the standards' conditions and at the same OD for CAPTURE-Screen. These zero-concentration readings establish the calibration baseline and blank scatter for LOD; on CAPTURE-Screen, clean cell blanks also establish the scatter baseline.
Buffer-only cuvette
Used for the CAPTURE-Screen Instrument self-check. It does not replace the cell blank or set the cell-scatter baseline.

With OD normalization enabled, the Plate Reader Assay uses (F_sample − mean F_medium) / (OD_sample − mean OD_medium). This implementation still needs to be confirmed against the wet lab's protocol (Table 6).

1Plan the standards

You do

Prepare standards of known concentration, and blanks with none.

LasReader does

  • Asks for at least 4 concentrations and 2 blanks, the minimum for a four-parameter curve and a limit of detection.
  • On CAPTURE-Screen, interleaves concentrations in replicate rounds to reduce the time bias from reading all replicates of one concentration together; drift may still affect concentrations differently.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen → Calibrate

2Read

You do

Read the plate on any plate reader, or the tubes on CAPTURE-Screen.

LasReader does

  • Takes the plate as the reader exports it (Figure 2), with fluorescence divided by OD600 if you choose.
  • On CAPTURE-Screen, reads each tube in one click and subtracts the dark for you (Figure 4).

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen

3Check the data

You do

Make sure the instrument behaves and no reading is off.

LasReader does

  • Self-checks CAPTURE-Screen with a buffer-only cuvette, and flags any tube that saturated, scattered more than the blank, or was read under other settings. The scatter check matters: a culture without the reporter can still read a few counts once it scatters enough, so the scatter channel has to be read beside the green one (Engineering, reader cycle 2).
  • Excludes a reading only with a written reason, and keeps it in the export.

In CAPTURE-Screen → Instrument, and every fit

4Fit the curve

You do

Draw a standard curve through the standards.

LasReader does

  • Fits a weighted four-parameter logistic curve[1] and works out the limits of detection and quantification[2][3] (§2.1).
  • Marks the usable range: the only part of the curve that returns a number.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen

5Convert the samples

You do

Read each sample's concentration off the curve.

LasReader does

  • Averages the replicates, converts once, scales by the dilution, and gives an approximate 95% confidence interval.
  • Below the usable range or above its upper limit, reports a bound instead of a concentration estimate.

In Plate Reader Assay and CAPTURE-Screen → Measure

6Compare instruments

You do

Measure the same samples on a commercial plate reader and on CAPTURE-Screen.

LasReader does

  • Pairs the results by sample name and shows how well they agree: a log-log plot, the geometric mean ratio and Bland–Altman limits of agreement[4] (§2.1).

In Cross-Validation

7Export and reproduce

You do

Record the results so that someone else can check them.

LasReader does

  • Exports a CSV with every reading, every exclusion and its reason, and every result, plus the curve as a PNG. CAPTURE-Screen backs everything up to one JSON file.
  • Because every reading and exclusion is in the file, anyone can enter them again and get the same curve.

Part 2

How it decides

How a reading becomes a concentration, and how sure that number is: a weighted four-parameter fit, limits set from the blanks, and an approximate 95% confidence interval on every result. Every equation below is what the code computes; the full derivation is in the repository's model document.

2.1The method, step by step

The same five steps run in both tools, in the order the data flows through them. Each step gives its equations, defines every symbol, and names the section of the model document that derives it.

  1. Model the response
  2. Weight and fit
  3. Set the limits
  4. Invert, with an interval
  5. Compare instruments

1Model the response

A biosensor saturates, so its signal is S-shaped on a log concentration axis. LasReader uses the four-parameter logistic (4PL)[1], whose parameters each mean something at the bench.

F(c; θ) = B + {{ frac('T − B', '1 + (EC50 / c)h') }}, θ = (T, B, ln EC50, h) (1) where B is the signal with no target, T the saturated signal, EC50 the concentration at half response and h the Hill slope. Blanks enter at c = 0, where F = B, so they fix the baseline directly. Fitting ln EC50 keeps EC50 positive; h is held between 0.05 and 20.

Model document §7

2Weight and fit

Replicates scatter more at high signal than at low, so an unweighted fit would let the top standards dominate. LasReader measures how the scatter grows from the replicates themselves and weights each reading by the inverse of its variance.

σ2(F) = a + bF2 (2) An additive part a and a proportional part b, found by least squares from each concentration's replicate variance against its squared mean, with a, b ≥ 0.
θ̂ = arg minθ Σi {{ frac('(yi − F(ci; θ))2', 'σi2') }}, σi2 = max(si2, a + byi2) (3) where yi is reading i at concentration ci, and si its own read-noise SD (CAPTURE-Screen measures it on every read; it is 0 for a plate reader). The sum is minimised by Levenberg–Marquardt. The read noise alone would understate the scatter: on the first CAPTURE-Screen prototype, seating and preparing the tube outweighed the noise of the reading itself in four of seven replicate groups (Model 3.1).
Σθ = {{ frac('χ2min', 'N − 4') }} (J⊤WJ)−1 (4) The uncertainty of the fitted curve: J is the N × 4 matrix of derivatives ∂F/∂θ at θ̂, W = diag(1/σi2), N the number of readings and χ2min the minimised sum in (3). With one reading per concentration and no read-noise estimate, the fit is unweighted and a is taken from the residuals.

Model document §8, §9

3Set the limits

A curve can only be read where it is both above the noise of the blanks and still rising. LasReader works out that stretch from the data and never extrapolates beyond it.

LOD = F−1(F̄0 + 3s0), LOQ = F−1(F̄0 + 10s0) (5) where F̄0 is the larger of the blanks' mean and B, s0 the blanks' standard deviation, and F−1 the inverse curve of (7). The blank mean plus a multiple of the blank SD is the usual way to set both limits[2]; 10 is IUPAC's default multiplier for quantification[3].
cmin = max(LOD, clowest standard), cmax = min(chighest standard, EC50·191/h) (6) EC50·191/h is where the curve reaches 95% of its response span: setting F = B + 0.95(T − B) in (1) gives (EC50/c)h = 1/19. The upper-end region beyond 95% of the response span is excluded because inversion becomes sensitive to small signal changes. Outside [cmin, cmax], only a bound is reported, with no concentration estimate.

The lower bound is the larger of the LOD and the lowest non-zero standard concentration. A sample can therefore be above the LOD but below the usable range. The current app's below_lod status covers both cases; it does not always mean the signal is below the detection limit.

The usable range is the software's permitted inversion range, not an experimentally validated quantification range. Its lower bound uses LOD, not LOQ, so estimates may be returned between LOD and LOQ. The blank-based LOQ estimate alone does not establish reliable quantification: bias, precision and recovery still need to be checked with low-concentration samples.

Model document §10

4Invert, with an interval

A sample's replicates are averaged in signal first, then converted once: the curve is non-linear, so averaging converted concentrations would be biased. The interval is worked out on the log scale, because concentration errors are multiplicative.

ln ĉ = ln EC50 + {{ frac('1', 'h') }} ln {{ frac('F̄ − B', 'T − F̄') }} (7) where F̄ is the mean of the sample's n replicate readings.
Var(ln ĉ) ≈ gF2 {{ frac('a + bF̄2', 'n') }} + gθ⊤ Σθ gθ, approximate 95% CI = exp(ln ĉ ± 1.96√Var) (8) The first term is the noise of the reading, which shrinks as n grows; the second is the uncertainty of the curve, from (4), which every sample shares and more replicates cannot reduce. This is the delta method: a first-order expansion of (7).
gF = {{ frac('1', 'h') }} ( {{ frac('1', 'F̄ − B') }} + {{ frac('1', 'T − F̄') }} ), gθ = ( −{{ frac('1', 'h(T − F̄)') }}, −{{ frac('1', 'h(F̄ − B)') }}, 1, −{{ frac('1', 'h2') }} ln {{ frac('F̄ − B', 'T − F̄') }} ) (9) The derivatives of (7) with respect to the signal and to the four parameters in θ, in the order of (1).

Model document §11, §12

5Compare instruments

Cross-Validation asks whether CAPTURE-Screen agrees with a commercial plate reader on the same samples. Both tools run the same fitting code, curve_fit.js, which reduces differences caused by separate analysis implementations. Differences can still arise from the instruments, sample handling, OD normalization, calibration and operation. Agreement is measured on the log scale, as a ratio:

di = log10 {{ frac('ĉiCAPTURE', 'ĉiplate') }}, GMR = 10d, LoA95 = [10d − 1.96sd, 10d + 1.96sd] (10) where d and sd are the mean and standard deviation of di over the paired samples. The geometric mean ratio (GMR) is the typical bias; the Bland–Altman limits of agreement (LoA)[4] are the range 95% of single comparisons fall in. The tool also counts the pairs whose approximate 95% CIs overlap.

Assumptions. Replicate readings are independent, with roughly normal errors; the response rises with concentration (T > B); and the interval in (8) is a first-order approximation with 1.96. Its coverage has not been validated and may be inaccurate with few tubes or strong non-linearity. These are listed with the other limits in Table 6.

Steps 1–4 can be tried on your own readings in §3.2, which runs the same code.

2.2Rules it keeps

§2.1 says how a number is computed. These rules say when LasReader refuses to give one, so a result is never more certain than the data behind it. Each is enforced by the code, not left to the person at the bench.

Before the fit

R1No curve from too little data
With fewer than 4 standard concentrations or 2 blanks, the fit will not run. Plate Reader Assay, CAPTURE-Screen
R2Nothing disappears silently
Quality flags warn and leave the decision to you; the one exception is a reading taken under other settings, which a curve never uses (R4). A reading is excluded only with a written reason, and stays on the plot and in the export. Plate Reader Assay, CAPTURE-Screen

When it gives a number

R3No number outside the usable range
Outside cmin to cmax (§2.1, step 3), only the bound is reported, never an extrapolated value. Plate Reader Assay, CAPTURE-Screen
R4A curve converts only what it was made for
Each curve is bound to the reader's configuration fingerprint (e4c7f2 on the step bar, Figure 3). Readings taken any other way are not converted with it. CAPTURE-Screen

Over time

R5A saved curve never changes
Refitting makes a new curve, so every result can be traced to the curve that produced it. CAPTURE-Screen
R6No demo data
With CAPTURE-Screen off, the pages say it is offline. They never show made-up readings. All tools

2.3How the pieces connect

LasReader has three parts: the reader, a small backend, and the pages in your browser. All of the fluorescence analysis runs in the browser; the backend only relays the reader and analyses plate photos.

Part 3

For other fluorescent biosensors

The analysis does not depend on AHL. A team whose fluorescent biosensor has a rising response suitable for 4PL calibration, and who can make standards of known concentration, can use the Plate Reader Assay and check the fit against their own experimental data. Table 1 lists what other setups would change.

3.1Who can use it

LasReader was built for our AHL biosensor, so its pages say "AHL" and its labels and export columns say nM. The analysis never uses either: it fits standards of known concentration to their readings and converts samples through that curve. Any unit works, as long as standards and samples share it; keep a note of yours, since the labels still say nM.

What it does need is at least 4 standard concentrations and 2 blank readings, and a rising signal suitable for a 4PL calibration. Check that the fitted curve describes the experimental response before interpreting sample estimates.

3.5Run it, build on it

Most teams only need the first path below. The other two are for running your own copy and for connecting a CAPTURE-Screen. HIA-Taiwan, a high-school iGEM team, has already asked us about the code's programming structure (Collaboration).

1Use it in the browser

Nothing to install: open the live app and every tool is there. The Plate Reader Assay and Cross-Validation run entirely in your browser; CAPTURE-Screen and Plate Imaging · Agar Vision also use our server, which holds one reader at a time (path 3 runs your own).

2Run it on your own computer

For working offline or changing the code. You need Git and Python 3.14 (check with python --version); the frontend has no build step and needs no Node.js.

Run setup once to install the pinned packages into backend/.venv; dev starts the backend and frontend together.

macOS or Linux (on Windows, the same lines work in Git Bash):

git clone https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/ncku-tainan/lasreader.git
cd lasreader
bash scripts/setup.sh
bash scripts/dev.sh

Windows PowerShell:

git clone https://gitlab.igem.org/2026/software/ncku-tainan/lasreader.git
cd lasreader
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File .\scripts\setup.ps1
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File .\scripts\dev.ps1

Then open http://127.0.0.1:5500 in your browser. Press Ctrl+C in the terminal to stop.

For developers: the API documents itself at http://127.0.0.1:8000/docs, and in backend/, .venv/bin/python -m pytest (.venv\Scripts\python -m pytest on Windows) runs the 46 backend tests.

3Connect your own CAPTURE-Screen

Our online server holds one reader at a time, so a team with its own reader (Table 1) runs LasReader on a lab computer on the same Wi-Fi. Do the setup in path 2, then, instead of dev:

  1. In backend/, start the backend so the reader can reach it: .venv/bin/python -m uvicorn app.main:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000 (.venv\Scripts\python on Windows).
  2. In frontend/, in a second terminal: python -m http.server 5500, and open http://127.0.0.1:5500.
  3. In the reader's secrets.h, set BACKEND_HOST to this computer's address on the Wi-Fi, BACKEND_PORT to 8000 and BACKEND_USE_TLS to 0, and flash it. The rest of the reader's setup is in the README's Hardware section.

Part 4

Where it stands

LasReader runs end to end on real instruments: a commercial plate reader, our own reader at the bench, and photos of real plates, where its measurements agree with a ruler to within 0.71 mm on average. What the fluorescence tools cannot show yet is a validated concentration, because that needs a biosensor response to calibrate against.

Plate Imaging · Agar Vision

A separate tool for plate phenotypes: the size and spread of colonies, such as a swarming colony, measured from photos of the plates.

Input
Plate photos taken from above, up to 24 per batch, each with its strain, condition and replicate.
Scale
The plate is found in the photo, and its known diameter (93 mm by default, adjustable) turns pixels into millimetres.
Detection
The background is corrected, colonies are thresholded, and a region that looks like more than one colony is split. Photos on a purple UV background are detected and handled separately.
Output
Each colony's longest span, equivalent diameter and area, in one CSV for the batch, and every photo returned annotated so you can check the plate edge was found.
Reproducible
The analysis is deterministic: the same photos and plate diameter give the same numbers. The CSV records both on every row.

The analysis is Tzu-Chiao Chou's colony-measurement code, run unchanged on the backend in Python with OpenCV. The backend keeps neither the photos nor the results.
