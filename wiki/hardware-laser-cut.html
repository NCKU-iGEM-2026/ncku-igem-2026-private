The instrument

A blue LED, a 90° sensor and a light trap, in a plywood box cut from one sheet.

A blue LED lights a UVette from the side, and an AS7341 spectral sensor looks at it from 90°. Everything sits in a laser-cut plywood enclosure in two layers: the optics above, blackened with matte black marker, and the ESP32 and its wiring below, with a deck between them. An OLED shows each result and one button starts a measurement.

The light trap is designed to swallow the excitation that passes straight through the sample so that it does not bounce back toward the sensor.

The data show the sensor sits to the side of the beam. With no cuvette in place, the blue channel F3 (480 nm) read about 312 counts; with a cuvette of plain medium it rose to about 730 (both in the fluorescence test). A reading that goes up when the sample goes in is consistent with a sensor collecting light scattered sideways out of the sample.

How a reading is made

Each reading subtracts LED-off from LED-on, and the result is the ratio F5/F3, which cancels changes in how much light reaches the sensor.

For each reading the firmware takes eight samples with the LED off, then eight with it on, and subtracts the averages channel by channel, which removes ambient light and the sensor's dark current; averaging eight samples also lowers the sensor's own noise by about √8, or 2.8 times. One press of the button records a batch of five readings, about 22 seconds each. The first is discarded and the other four are averaged.

The figure we report is F5/F3: the green channel at 555 nm divided by the blue channel at 480 nm. Dividing by the blue channel largely cancels changes in how much light reaches the sensor, whether from where the cuvette sits or from changes within a batch: over readings two to five, F3 and F5 each fall by about 2.7%, while F5/F3 changes by less than 0.1% on average. Net signal is the F5/F3 of a tube minus that of a tube with no sfGFP cells, measured in the same session.

Why F5, and not F4

F4, centred nearer the sfGFP peak, collects about 2.2 times as much sfGFP light as F5, but it also collects about three times the background from the blue LED, so its detection limit is slightly worse (about 4.4% against 3.6%). On this build F5/F3 is the better measure of sfGFP; F4/F3 supports the same conclusions with more noise.

F5 against F4, in full the same data, both ratios Show Hide

sfGFP emits most strongly near 510 nm. F4 and F5 each respond over a band 39 nm wide at half maximum (AS7341 datasheet): F4 from about 496 to 535 nm, F5 from about 536 to 575 nm. The band centred closest to the sfGFP peak is F4. This build was set up from the start to read F5 (555 nm): the on-screen signal of the firmware used for these measurements is built on it. To check that choice we recomputed the main results with F4/F3 alongside, using the same data and the same method.

F4/F3 supports the same conclusions, linear and with an intercept indistinguishable from zero. Where it does better is stability: it shifts less when the same tube is taken out and put back, and drifts less over an hour, because F4 and F3 are close in wavelength. For sensitivity, F5/F3 is the better measure on this build.

The other number in our records: SIG5 and k5 not used for any result Show Hide

For its on-screen reading the firmware also computes SIG5 = F5 − k5 × F3. Part of what F5 sees is not sfGFP fluorescence, such as the blue LED's long-wave tail scattered into the sensor; k5 is that part as a fraction of F3, measured on a tube with no sfGFP cells. It is set by sending the k command with such a tube in place, and stored in the ESP32's flash.

k5 changes from one experiment to the next, so SIG5 is not compared across batches. Every result on this page uses F5/F3, which does not depend on k5.

Validation

The LED holds steady, and with the sensor aligned the reader detects sfGFP down to about 4% sfGFP culture; our AHL sensor strain's signal is too faint for it.

The density test: does the reading follow the sample?

Aim Check that the reading follows what is in the cuvette.
Method Cultures of different density (OD600 0.15 to 1.53) were read one after another, and the blue channel F3 of the 28 batches with an OD600 record was compared with their OD600.

Result Culture density explained almost none of the variation in the blue channel F3 (R² = 0.01): the reading was not following what was in the cuvette, consistent with a sensor mounted too low to see the sample well. The reading also fell by about 40% in a single step between the seventh and eighth batches, about 20 minutes in, and stayed low. Allowing for culture density, a single step fits the data much better than a gradual drift (R² 0.88 against 0.67). The cause of the step was not identified, but a sudden change like this is more consistent with the sensor shifting than with the LED fading. The test gave no usable culture data. The next test checked whether the LED was at fault; after that, the sensor was aligned with the cuvette's measuring window and fixed in place for all later tests.

The LED stability test: an hour with nothing moved

Aim Find out whether the LED itself fades, which could have caused the fall seen in the density test.
Method With the sensor still in its original position and the chamber empty, the firmware repeated batches automatically for an hour: five readings taking about two minutes, then a one-minute pause with the LED off. Twenty-one batches in all.

Result and conclusion Over an hour the reading stayed within a 1% range. The LED and electronics are stable, so the fall in the density test was not the LED fading. The sensor was then aligned with the cuvette's measuring window and fixed in place for every test that followed.

The fluorescence test: is the signal sfGFP?

Aim Show that the signal comes from sfGFP fluorescence and scales with the amount of sfGFP.
Method Twelve tubes of 200 µL in a grid: culture density d of 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 (the fraction of each tube that is culture, the rest medium) crossed with a fraction f of 0, 0.5 and 1 of cells carrying sfGFP. The two stock cultures had nearly the same density (OD600 0.84 with sfGFP, 0.85 without), so the share of culture taken from the sfGFP stock is close to the share of sfGFP cells. Each tube was read in at least two batches.

Result Net F5/F3 rose in proportion to the amount of sfGFP, d × f (r² ≈ 0.90, eight tubes), and the line passes through zero (intercept −0.5 ± 2.2, in net F5/F3 × 1000).

Conclusion The signal behaves as sfGFP fluorescence: it is proportional to the amount of sfGFP and has the spectral shape of its emission.

Why this is not scattering the spectral check Show Hide

A signal that rises with the amount of sfGFP does not on its own prove fluorescence. Each net value already compares tubes of the same density, so turbidity is subtracted, but the two strains could still scatter light differently. The spectral shape can tell scattering from fluorescence, and it is shown under What ten channels add: the difference between tubes with and without sfGFP has the shape expected of sfGFP fluorescence.

The linearity test: a straight line and a detection limit

Aim Keep the total culture density nearly constant, vary only the share of sfGFP culture, and measure the detection limit.
Method Tubes of 200 µL mixed to shares f of 0, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50, 0.75 and 1, where f is the share of the volume taken from the sfGFP culture, which is proportional to the number of sfGFP cells. The OD600 of every tube in the fit was measured, from 1.30 (no sfGFP) to 1.45 (all sfGFP). Each tube was read in at least two batches.

Result Signal and the share of sfGFP culture are linear. Without forcing the line through the origin, the intercept is 1.6 ± 2.6 (standard error, in net F5/F3 × 1000), not different from zero, with r² ≈ 0.97; a line through the origin has slope 68.7 and r² ≈ 0.97. The detection limit, three times the standard deviation of the blank tube over its four placements divided by the slope, is about 4% sfGFP culture (from F5/F3).

The low end why the lowest shares scatter Show Hide

At the lowest shares the points scatter most relative to their size: the two lowest tubes in the fit are off by −23% (f = 0.10) and +48% (f = 0.15), while from f = 0.20 up every tube is within about ±20%. At f = 0.05 a 200 µL tube holds the equivalent of only 10 µL of sfGFP culture, so an error of 2 µL is already 20% of f; the low tubes are the most sensitive to how they were mixed. The reading itself is not the cause: repeated batches of the same tube agree, the zero is stable, and F4, F5 and F6 are off in the same direction and by similar amounts in each tube (correlation 0.95–0.98), so the deviation lies in how much sfGFP the tube holds, not in one channel. Three further low-share tubes (two at f = 0.05, one at f = 0.10) scatter the same way, from −54% to +276%, and are shown but left out of the fit. Including them changes little: the slope becomes 68.8 and r² 0.95, and the detection limit, which comes from the blank tube, does not change. Mixing low shares by serial dilution should reduce this scatter.

The sensor-strain test: our AHL sensor on this reader

Aim Read the AHL-sensing strain our wet lab built, E. coli TOP10 carrying ProD-LasR · pLasRV-sfGFP, across a range of AHL.
Method The strain was given AHL at six concentrations, 10 nM to 1 mM, with two or three readings at each, 16 in all, and three tubes of medium alone as blanks. It was read with the current firmware rather than the batch firmware of the tests above.

Result F5/F3 stayed between 0.048 and 0.060 (mean 0.055) across the whole range, at the level of the culture without sfGFP in the linearity test (0.055) and far below the undiluted sfGFP test culture (0.127); medium alone read 0.026 to 0.033. The differences between concentrations were no larger than those between tubes at one concentration. F4 rose and fell with the red channel F7 and the clear channel (r = 0.99 and 0.92): the readings followed the light scattered by the cells.

Conclusion This reader detects and quantifies a strongly expressing sfGFP culture down to about 4%, but our sensor strain's F5/F3 could not be told apart from a culture without sfGFP read in another session. No culture without sfGFP was read alongside it, so the result cannot be stated against the 4% detection limit. In the wet lab's plate reader the strain did glow above its control (Engineering), so the signal exists but is too faint for this reader. The 3D-printed build saw the same pattern with dense suspensions: readings followed the amount of culture, not the AHL (Where it stops).

What ten channels add

The extra channels confirm that the signal is fluorescence, and that the ratio cancels where the cuvette sits.

Every reading records all ten channels, while the results above use only F5 and F3. Recomputing the later tests with the other channels checks those results from a different angle, without another experiment. The conclusions hold up; these are the two checks that matter most.

The added signal has the shape of fluorescence

For each culture density in the fluorescence test we took the tube with all cells carrying sfGFP and the tube with none, divided each by its own F3 to remove brightness differences, and subtracted. What is left is the spectrum that sfGFP adds. Because every channel is divided by F3, the F3 point is zero by construction; each curve is then scaled to its own F4 value so that the four densities can be compared by shape.

The four curves almost coincide: a single peak at F4 (515 nm), falling steadily toward the red, and near zero at 415 nm. That is the shape expected of sfGFP emission. Scattering would be strongest at short wavelengths, and a change in turbidity would move every channel the same way; neither would produce this curve.

The ratio cancels where the cuvette sits

If only the amount of light reaching the sensor changes, all channels change in proportion and the ratio is unaffected. The figure shows a real case from the fluorescence test: a blank was read, taken out while another tube was read, then put back and read again.

When the blank went back in, the nine channels rose together by 14 to 20%, at most 6% apart, while F5/F3 moved by 2.9%: the jump came from where the cuvette sat, and the ratio largely cancelled it.

Limits and next steps

  • It does not measure turbidity. The 90° geometry suits fluorescence and does not track culture density; no choice of channels recovers it. Cell density has to be measured separately, with a spectrophotometer.
  • Low shares scatter more. Below about 20% sfGFP culture, tubes lie further from the line (see The low end); mixing them by serial dilution should help but has not been tested.
  • Experiments are not directly comparable. The reader was moved between sessions, so absolute counts differ from one experiment to the next (an empty chamber read about 310 in the fluorescence test and 160 to 190 in the linearity test). Each experiment is therefore referenced to its own tube with no sfGFP, measured in the same session.
  • The AHL sensor strain is too faint for this reader. Our sensor strain glows above its control in a plate reader, but on this reader its F5/F3 stayed at the level of cells without sfGFP, read in another session, from 10 nM to 1 mM AHL; no culture without sfGFP was read alongside it (see the sensor-strain test).
  • No reference comparison yet. This reader has not been compared with a plate reader.

The laser-cut parts

The whole set of parts is generated in the browser from the measured thickness of the plywood. The optical layout stays fixed: the cuvette pocket, the heights and positions of the light holes, and the inside of every chamber do not change with thickness, and thicker board makes the walls grow outward. Tabs are 8 mm wide; slots and notches are made 0.1 mm smaller on each side to take up the laser's kerf. The jointed parts all fit one 450 × 300 mm sheet and must be cut from one batch of board, because every joint is sized from a single measured thickness. The small parts without joints fill the gaps around them; those that do not fit go on a second sheet of any board.

Assembly, step by step 10 steps Show Hide

This is the order our reader was put together in. Open the chambers in the 3D view to see each part named here.

  1. Cut and colour. Cut the parts for your board's thickness, the jointed ones from one sheet. Blacken every surface facing the optical layer with matte black marker; the electronics layer can stay bare.
  2. Chambers. Press the walls' feet into the deck slots, the 12 mm wall included; friction holds them.
  3. Light trap. Glue the two triangles against its long walls and the square onto their slopes, lower edge against the wall with the 8 mm opening.
  4. LED. Push the LED into the 4 mm hole in the cuvette wall, tape it in place, and take its leads down through the round wire hole.
  5. Sensor. Glue the support plate under the deck's sensor slot, stand the sensor board in the slot facing the cuvette, and take its wires down through the rectangular wire hole.
  6. Enclosure and rails. Glue the base and sides together, glue the four rails at about half height, and tape the breadboard with the ESP32 to the base.
  7. Deck. Connect all wires to the ESP32 as in the wiring table, then lower the deck onto the rails.
  8. Display and button. Lead their wires out through the long side's opening, connect them, and tape both over the opening.
  9. Roofs. Press the three roofs into the wall-top notches.
  10. Plate and lid. Lay the plate over the chambers, its 13 × 13 mm opening over the cuvette pocket, and put the lid on; neither is glued. Glue the 25 mm square under the 27 mm one to make the cover for the lid's opening.

Assembly tip: the deck and the enclosure panels have symmetric outlines, so each also fits when turned around, but their holes are not symmetric and the parts carry no orientation marks. Before gluing, check that every hole lines up with what it serves: the LED, the cuvette, the sensor and the wire holes.

Operating steps 6 steps Show Hide

These are the steps the first four tests under Validation were taken with, using the batch firmware described under Firmware and data.

  1. Connect. Plug the ESP32 into a computer by USB and open a serial monitor at 115200 baud. The display shows the reader's status and “Type ID, press button”.
  2. Load. Fill a UVette with the sample and lower it through the lid opening into its pocket, the 2 mm path toward the LED. Close the opening with the small cover.
  3. Calibrate. Start each session with a tube with no sfGFP. Load it and run one batch first, then type k into the serial monitor. The display shows CAL OK with k5, the share of the blue signal that reaches the green channel. This only sets the on-screen SIG; it does not affect F5/F3.
  4. Name and run. Type the tube's ID into the serial monitor and press the button. The reader takes five readings, about 22 seconds each; leave it alone while the display says “do not touch”.
  5. Read. The display shows DONE with the mean F3 and SIG of readings two to five. Every reading also goes to the serial monitor as a line of CSV with all ten channels; F5/F3, the figure used on this page, is computed from those lines.
  6. Reference. Read the tube with no sfGFP again during the session. Every result on this page is a tube's F5/F3 minus that tube's.

The circuit

Everything runs from the ESP32's 3.3 V supply. The AS7341 and the OLED share one I²C bus at different addresses (0x39 and 0x3C); both boards carry their own pull-up resistors. The LED is driven directly: GPIO25 to 220 Ω to the LED to ground, switched by the firmware. The button connects GPIO13 to ground and uses the ESP32's internal pull-up. None of the pins used (13, 21, 22, 25) is a strapping pin that affects boot. The ESP32 itself is powered over USB, through the opening in one short side of the enclosure: we use a power bank, or the computer when the batch firmware sends its readings, but any USB supply will do.

Firmware and data

The readings on this page were taken with the reader's batch firmware (v5.5), which prints every reading over the serial port. The current firmware builds on it and adds the link to our LasReader software: a measurement still takes five readings and keeps the last four, and the data processing is done in the software rather than on the reader. It is published in the team's GitLab repository; the software is described on the Software page.
