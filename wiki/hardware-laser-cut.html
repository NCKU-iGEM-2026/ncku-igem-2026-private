The laser-cut build

Over four sessions between 2026-09-16 and 09-22, this build took 600 readings of E. coli cultures with and without GFP. Its green-to-blue ratio rose in a straight line with the fraction of GFP cells (r² ≈ 0.97), resolved down to about 3% GFP cells, and drifted by about 1% in an hour. Across its ten channels the added signal has the spectral shape of fluorescence, not of scattering. It has not been compared against a plate reader, so nothing here is a claim of absolute accuracy.

The instrument

A blue LED lights a UVette from the side, and an AS7341 spectral sensor looks at it from 90°. Everything sits in a laser-cut enclosure of blackened plywood in two layers: the optics above, the ESP32 and its wiring below, with a light-tight deck between them. An OLED shows each result and one button starts a measurement.

Excitation
Cree C503B blue LED, 470 nm, driven from GPIO25 through 220 Ω
Detector
AS7341, ten channels (DFRobot Gravity SEN0364 board), I²C 0x39
Sensor settings
Gain 512×, ATIME 59, ASTEP 999: 166.8 ms per integration, full scale 60,000 counts. Unchanged across all four sessions
Cuvette
Eppendorf UVette, 200 µL. The 2 mm path faces the LED, the 10 mm path faces the sensor
Emission filter
None. Blue is kept out by the 90° geometry and the light trap
Light trap
A deep, dark chamber straight across from the LED, entered through an 8 mm hole in the cuvette wall
Controller
ESP32 DevKit (ESP-WROOM-32), with an SSD1306 OLED and a push button
Enclosure
Laser-cut 3 mm plywood, blackened; two layers
Data
Four sessions, 2026-09-16 to 09-22, 600 readings

The light trap is designed to swallow the excitation that passes straight through the sample so that it does not bounce back toward the sensor. How well it does that has not been measured on its own.

The data show the sensor is not looking into the LED. With no cuvette in place, the blue channel F3 (480 nm) read about 312 counts, 0.5% of full scale; a sensor facing the LED would saturate. With a cuvette of plain medium it rose to about 730, which is consistent with a sensor collecting light scattered sideways out of the sample.

Where the data come from. Every reading on this part of the page was taken by the laser-cut build, inside its own enclosure with the lid closed. It is a separate instrument from the 3D-printed build: its firmware, sensor settings and counts are its own, and the two sets of data are not combined anywhere.

How a reading is made

For each reading the firmware takes eight samples with the LED off, then eight with it on, and subtracts the averages channel by channel, which removes ambient light and the sensor's dark current. One press of the button records a batch of five readings; the first is discarded, because it is taken just after the cuvette goes in, and the other four are averaged.

The figure we report is F5/F3: the green channel at 555 nm divided by the blue channel at 480 nm. Dividing by the blue channel cancels changes in how much light reaches the sensor, for example from where the cuvette sits. Net signal is the F5/F3 of a tube minus that of a tube with no GFP cells, measured the same day.

Why F5, and not F4

GFP emits most strongly near 510 nm, and the closest channel is F4 at 515 nm. F5 was the channel this build was set up to read from the start: the firmware's live signal, below, is built on it. To check that choice we recomputed every result with F4/F3 alongside, using the same data and the same method.

F4 does collect more GFP light, about 2.2 times as much as F5. But it sits next to the 470 nm excitation and also collects its long-wave tail: its background is three times that of F5 and varies more from batch to batch. The noise grows about fourfold, so the detection limit is twice as poor. F4/F3 supports every conclusion below, linear and with an intercept indistinguishable from zero, only with more noise. Its one advantage is that it shifts less when the cuvette is moved, because F4 and F3 are close in wavelength. On this build, F5/F3 is the better measure of GFP.

The other number in our records: SIG5 and k5 not used for any result Show Hide

For its live display the firmware also computes SIG5 = F5 − k5 × F3. Part of what F5 sees is the blue LED's long-wave tail rather than fluorescence; k5 is that tail as a fraction of F3. It is set by placing a tube with no GFP cells, sending the k command, and storing k5 = F5 ÷ F3 in the ESP32's flash.

k5 was 0.05358 (the default) on 09-16 and 09-17, was reset twice on 09-20 (0.05085, then 0.07772), and was 0.05756 on 09-22. Because it changes between days and even within one, SIG5 cannot be compared across batches. Every result on this page uses F5/F3, which does not depend on k5.

Four sessions

2026-09-16: the first culture test finds a problem

Aim. Put cultures of different density in and see whether the reader can tell them apart. Method. 32 batches: 28 with an OD600 record, from 22 different tubes (some read twice), plus 3 blanks and one batch with no OD600 recorded. The 28 were compared with their OD600.

Result. The reading was essentially unrelated to culture density (r = +0.09). The sensor was mounted too low, and what it received was mostly stray light scattered around inside the enclosure rather than light from the sample. The day produced no usable data, but it pinned down what to change: raise the sensor and close off the light path.

2026-09-17: the reading holds steady

Aim. After the light path was rebuilt, check whether the reading drifts on its own; a reader that drifts cannot be used quantitatively. Method. One sample left untouched, one batch about every three minutes for an hour, compared with the same test before the change.

Result and conclusion. The earlier decline came from how the light path was mounted, not from the LED ageing or failing. Once that was fixed the reading was stable enough for quantitative work.

2026-09-20: the signal is GFP fluorescence

Aim. Show that the signal comes from GFP fluorescence and scales with the amount of GFP. Method. A grid of samples varying both the culture density d and the fraction f of cells carrying GFP, read tube by tube: 57 batches, 285 readings.

Result. Net F5/F3 rose in proportion to the amount of GFP, d × f (r² ≈ 0.95 over twelve tubes). Lifting the cuvette out and putting it back changed the raw reading by about 14%; F5/F3 changed by only 2.9%, so the ratio cancels placement.

A signal that rises with the amount of GFP does not on its own prove fluorescence, because turbidity and scattering can rise too. The spectral shape can tell them apart, and it is shown under What ten channels add: the difference between tubes with and without GFP is a fluorescence spectrum.

Conclusion. The reader's signal comes from GFP fluorescence and is proportional to the amount of GFP. It has not been compared with a plate reader or other reference instrument, so we do not claim absolute accuracy.

2026-09-22: linearity and detection limit

Aim. Hold the total culture density fixed, vary only the fraction of GFP cells, and measure the detection limit. Method. Tubes of equal turbidity (OD600 0.85 without GFP, 0.84 with) mixed to different GFP fractions: 33 batches, 165 readings.

Result. Signal and GFP fraction are linear. Without forcing the line through the origin, the intercept is 1.2 ± 2.6 (standard error), not different from zero, with r² ≈ 0.97; a line through the origin has slope 68.1 and r² ≈ 0.97. The detection limit is about 3% GFP cells (3σ, from F5/F3).

The low end. The tubes with the least GFP are off the line. The most likely cause is error in pipetting the very small volumes they needed, 10 to 30 µL. Repeated reads of the same tube agree, the zero is stable, and the three green channels F4, F5 and F6 are off by the same amount, which rules out the reading side of the instrument. That evidence cannot prove the pipetting itself; it is the most reasonable explanation. Mixing low fractions by serial dilution is expected to fix it.

What ten channels add

Every reading records all ten channels, while the results above use only F5 and F3. Recomputing with the other channels tests those results independently, without another experiment. All four days' conclusions hold up; these are the two checks that matter most.

The added signal has the shape of fluorescence

For each culture density on 09-20 we took the tube with all cells carrying GFP and the tube with none, divided each by its own F3 to remove brightness differences, and subtracted. What is left is the spectrum that GFP adds.

The four curves almost coincide: a single peak at F4 (515 nm), falling steadily toward the red, and near zero at 415 nm. That is the shape of fluorescence emission. Scattering would be strongest at short wavelengths, and a change in turbidity would move every channel the same way; neither produces this curve.

What the ratio protects against, and what it does not

If only the amount of light reaching the sensor changes, all channels change in proportion and the ratio is unaffected. If the light source itself changes position or angle, the channels change by different amounts and the ratio moves with them. The figure compares two real events: on 09-20 the same blank was lifted out and put back; on 09-22 someone knocked the LED's jumper lead and the board rebooted.

When the cuvette went back in, the nine channels rose together by 14 to 20%, at most 6% apart, and F5/F3 moved by 2.9%: that jump came from where the cuvette sat, and the ratio cancelled it. The LED event was different: the channels changed by amounts a factor of 2.5 apart, and F5/F3 read 44% low. The most likely cause is that the LED moved; it is the only event of its kind we have recorded, and the data cannot separate a change of position from a change in its connection.

The ratio's blind spot. F5/F3 cancels where the cuvette sits, but not a change at the light source, and the reader gives no warning when that happens. The check needs no new hardware: the firmware already reports all ten channels, so each batch can test whether they changed in proportion. In proportion means only the sample moved and the data stand; out of proportion means the light path changed and the batch is discarded. This check is not yet in the firmware.

Limits and next steps

  • It does not measure turbidity. The 90° geometry suits fluorescence and does not track culture density; no choice of channels recovers it. Cell density has to be measured separately, with a spectrophotometer.
  • Low fractions need serial dilution. Below about 10% GFP cells, the error in pipetting very small volumes swamps the signal.
  • A light-source check is still to be written. The ten-channel proportionality test above should run on every batch in the firmware.
  • Days are not directly comparable. Calibration, light path and culture batch differ between days; intensities are compared only within one day.
  • No reference comparison yet. The reader has not been compared with a plate reader, and it has not read an AHL-induced culture.

Building one

The laser-cut parts

The whole set of parts is generated in the browser from the measured thickness of the plywood. The optical layout stays fixed: the cuvette pocket, the heights and positions of the light holes, and the inside of every chamber do not change with thickness, and thicker board makes the walls grow outward. Tabs are 8 mm wide; slots and notches are made 0.1 mm smaller on each side to take up the laser's kerf. The jointed parts all fit one 450 × 300 mm sheet and must be cut from one batch of board; a few small parts without joints go on a second sheet of any board.

Not yet test-cut. Three dimensions changed in the generator have not been cut and fitted: the light-trap chamber's roof (31 to 30 mm wide), the cuvette wall facing the light trap (37 to 36.58 mm long), and the regenerated enclosure joints. The panels and the deck are symmetric and can go in rotated; there are no orientation marks yet. The 12 mm small wall has no matching slot and may be a leftover part.

Checked before publishing: across 2.80–3.20 mm every check passes and all jointed parts fit one sheet; at 3.25 mm the generator refuses to produce files. The six enclosure panels, assembled in 3D from the generated outlines, close with no gaps and no overlaps at zero fit offset for every thickness from 2.8 to 3.2 mm.

The circuit

Everything runs from the ESP32's 3.3 V supply. The AS7341 and the OLED share one I²C bus at different addresses (0x39 and 0x3C); both boards carry their own pull-up resistors. The LED is driven directly: GPIO25 to 220 Ω to the LED to ground, switched by the firmware. The button connects GPIO13 to ground and uses the ESP32's internal pull-up. None of the pins used (13, 21, 22, 25) is a strapping pin that affects boot.
