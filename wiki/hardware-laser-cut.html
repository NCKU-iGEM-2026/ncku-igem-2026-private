The instrument

A blue LED lights a UVette from the side, and an AS7341 spectral sensor looks at it from 90°. Everything sits in a laser-cut plywood enclosure, painted matte black inside, in two layers: the optics above, the ESP32 and its wiring below, with a deck between them. An OLED shows each result and one button starts a measurement.

The light trap is designed to swallow the excitation that passes straight through the sample so that it does not bounce back toward the sensor.

The data show the sensor sits to the side of the beam. With no cuvette in place, the blue channel F3 (480 nm) read about 312 counts; with a cuvette of plain medium it rose to about 730 (both in the grid session). A reading that goes up when the sample goes in is consistent with a sensor collecting light scattered sideways out of the sample.

Where the data come from. Every reading on this part of the page was taken by this build, inside its own enclosure with the lid closed.

How a reading is made

For each reading the firmware takes eight samples with the LED off, then eight with it on, and subtracts the averages channel by channel, which removes ambient light and the sensor's dark current; averaging eight samples also lowers the sensor's own noise by about √8, or 2.8 times. One press of the button records a batch of five readings, about 22 seconds each. The first is discarded for two reasons. The signal is highest at the start of each batch and falls by about 4% over its five readings, even with nothing moved, which is consistent with the LED dimming as it warms; and readings taken right after a cuvette goes in are less stable. The other four are averaged.

The figure we report is F5/F3: the green channel at 555 nm divided by the blue channel at 480 nm. Dividing by the blue channel largely cancels changes in how much light reaches the sensor, whether from where the cuvette sits or from the LED dimming within a batch: over readings two to five, F3 and F5 each fall by about 2.7%, while F5/F3 changes by less than 0.1% on average. Net signal is the F5/F3 of a tube minus that of a tube with no sfGFP cells, measured in the same session.

Why F5, and not F4

sfGFP emits most strongly near 510 nm. F4 and F5 each respond over a band 39 nm wide at half maximum (AS7341 datasheet): F4 from about 496 to 535 nm, F5 from about 536 to 575 nm. The band centred closest to the sfGFP peak is F4. This build was set up from the start to read F5 (555 nm): the on-screen signal of the firmware used for these measurements is built on it. To check that choice we recomputed the main results with F4/F3 alongside, using the same data and the same method.

F4 does collect more sfGFP light, about 2.2 times as much as F5. But it sits next to the 470 nm excitation and also collects its long-wave tail, so its background is about three times that of F5, and the scatter of the blank tube between placements is about 2.7 times larger in absolute terms. The extra signal and the extra noise nearly cancel: F4's detection limit is slightly worse than F5's (about 4.4% against 3.6%), so it brings no gain in sensitivity. F4/F3 supports the same conclusions, linear and with an intercept indistinguishable from zero, only with more noise. Where it does better is stability: it shifts less when the cuvette is moved and drifts less over an hour, because F4 and F3 are close in wavelength. On this build, F5/F3 is the better measure of sfGFP.

The other number in our records: SIG5 and k5 not used for any result Show Hide

For its on-screen reading the firmware also computes SIG5 = F5 − k5 × F3. Part of what F5 sees is not sfGFP fluorescence, such as the blue LED's long-wave tail scattered into the sensor; k5 is that part as a fraction of F3, measured on a tube with no sfGFP cells. It is set by sending the k command with such a tube in place, and stored in the ESP32's flash.

k5 was 0.05358, the firmware default, in the first culture test and the stability test. At the start of the grid session it was reset twice, to 0.05085 and then 0.07772, before any grid tube was measured; it was 0.05756 in the linearity series. Because it changes between experiments and even within one, SIG5 cannot be compared across batches. Every result on this page uses F5/F3, which does not depend on k5.

Validation

Each test below asks one question about the reader: does it see the sample, does the LED hold steady, is the signal sfGFP fluorescence, and how small a share of sfGFP culture can it detect.

The first culture test: a problem found

Aim. Check that the reading responds to what is in the cuvette, using cultures of different density (OD600 0.15 to 1.53) as the variable. Method. 32 batches: 28 with an OD600 record, from 22 different tubes (some read more than once), plus 3 blanks and one batch with no OD600 recorded. The 28 were compared with their OD600.

Result. Culture density explained almost none of the variation in the blue channel F3 (R² = 0.01): the reading was not following what was in the cuvette, consistent with a sensor mounted too low to see the sample well. The reading also fell by about 40% in a single step between the seventh and eighth batches, about 20 minutes in, and stayed low. Allowing for culture density, a single step fits the data much better than a gradual drift (R² 0.88 against 0.67). The cause of the step was not identified, but a sudden change like this is more consistent with the sensor shifting than with the LED fading. The test gave no usable culture data. The next test checked whether the LED was at fault; after that, the sensor was raised, in line with the cuvette, for all later tests.

The stability test: the LED holds steady

Aim. Find out whether the LED itself fades, which could have caused the fall seen in the first culture test. Method. With the sensor still in its original position and the chamber empty, the firmware repeated batches automatically for an hour: five readings taking about two minutes, then a one-minute pause with the LED off. Twenty-one batches in all.

Result and conclusion. Over an hour the reading stayed within a 1% range. The LED and electronics are stable, so the fall in the first culture test was not the LED fading. The sensor was then raised for every test that followed.

The grid: the signal is sfGFP fluorescence

Aim. Show that the signal comes from sfGFP fluorescence and scales with the amount of sfGFP. Method. Twelve tubes of 200 µL in a grid: culture density d of 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 (the fraction of each tube that is culture, the rest medium) crossed with a fraction f of 0, 0.5 and 1 of cells carrying sfGFP. The two stock cultures had nearly the same density (OD600 0.84 with sfGFP, 0.85 without), so the share of culture taken from the sfGFP stock is close to the share of sfGFP cells. Each tube was read in at least two batches. A thirteenth tube, a repeat of d 0.5, f 0.5, was read first, and one grid tube was read again at the end; neither is part of the grid analysis.

Result. Net F5/F3 rose in proportion to the amount of sfGFP, d × f (r² ≈ 0.90 over the eight tubes with sfGFP; the four tubes with f = 0 are each row's zero and are not part of the fit). Without forcing the line through the origin, the intercept is −0.5 ± 2.2 (net F5/F3 × 1000), not different from zero.

A signal that rises with the amount of sfGFP does not on its own prove fluorescence. Each net value already compares tubes of the same density, so turbidity is subtracted, but the two strains could still scatter light differently. The spectral shape can tell scattering from fluorescence, and it is shown under What ten channels add: the difference between tubes with and without sfGFP has the shape expected of sfGFP fluorescence.

Conclusion. The signal behaves as sfGFP fluorescence: it is proportional to the amount of sfGFP and has the spectral shape of its emission.

The linearity series: a straight line and a detection limit

Aim. Keep the total culture density nearly constant, vary only the share of sfGFP culture, and measure the detection limit. Method. Tubes of 200 µL mixed to shares f of 0, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50, 0.75 and 1, where f is the share of the volume taken from the sfGFP culture, which is proportional to the number of sfGFP cells. The OD600 of every tube in the fit was measured, from 1.30 (no sfGFP) to 1.45 (all sfGFP). Each tube was read in at least two batches. Three further low-f tubes (two at f = 0.05, one at f = 0.10) were also read but are not part of the fit; see the low end below.

Result. Signal and the share of sfGFP culture are linear. Without forcing the line through the origin, the intercept is 1.6 ± 2.6 (standard error, in net F5/F3 × 1000), not different from zero, with r² ≈ 0.97; a line through the origin has slope 68.7 and r² ≈ 0.97. The detection limit, three times the standard deviation of the blank tube over its four placements divided by the slope, is about 4% sfGFP culture (from F5/F3).

The low end. At the lowest shares the points scatter most relative to their size: the two lowest tubes in the fit are off by −23% (f = 0.10) and +48% (f = 0.15), while from f = 0.20 up every tube is within about ±20%. At f = 0.05 a 200 µL tube holds the equivalent of only 10 µL of sfGFP culture, so an error of 2 µL is already 20% of f; the low tubes are the most sensitive to how they were mixed. The reading itself is not the cause: repeated batches of the same tube agree, the zero is stable, and F4, F5 and F6 are off in the same direction and by similar amounts in each tube (correlation 0.95–0.98), so the deviation lies in how much sfGFP the tube holds, not in one channel. Three further low-share tubes (two at f = 0.05, one at f = 0.10) scatter the same way, from −54% to +276%, and are shown but left out of the fit. Including them changes little: the slope becomes 68.8 and r² 0.95, and the detection limit, which comes from the blank tube, does not change. Mixing low shares by serial dilution should reduce this scatter.

What ten channels add

Every reading records all ten channels, while the results above use only F5 and F3. Recomputing the later tests with the other channels checks those results from a different angle, without another experiment. The conclusions hold up; these are the two checks that matter most.

The added signal has the shape of fluorescence

For each culture density in the grid we took the tube with all cells carrying sfGFP and the tube with none, divided each by its own F3 to remove brightness differences, and subtracted. What is left is the spectrum that sfGFP adds. Because every channel is divided by F3, the F3 point is zero by construction; each curve is then scaled to its own F4 value so that the four densities can be compared by shape.

The four curves almost coincide: a single peak at F4 (515 nm), falling steadily toward the red, and near zero at 415 nm. That is the shape expected of sfGFP emission. Scattering would be strongest at short wavelengths, and a change in turbidity would move every channel the same way; neither would produce this curve.

What the ratio protects against, and what it does not

If only the amount of light reaching the sensor changes, all channels change in proportion and the ratio is unaffected. If the light path itself changes, for example in where the LED points, the channels can change by different amounts and the ratio moves with them. The figure compares two real events. In the grid session, before the grid tubes, a blank was read, taken out while another tube was read, then put back and read again. During the linearity series, the LED was disturbed and the board restarted.

When the blank went back in, the nine channels rose together by 14 to 20%, at most 6% apart, and F5/F3 moved by 2.9%: that jump came from where the cuvette sat, and the ratio largely cancelled it. After the LED was disturbed, the channels changed by amounts a factor of 2.5 apart, and F5/F3 read 44% low. This is the only such event we have recorded, and the data cannot tell whether the LED's position or its connection changed.

The ratio's blind spot. F5/F3 largely cancels where the cuvette sits, but not a change in the light path, and the reader gives no warning when that happens. A check needs no new hardware: the firmware already reports all ten channels, so a fixed reference, such as the same blank tube, can be read at intervals and compared with its previous reading. A change in proportion suggests only the cuvette's position changed and the data stand; a change out of proportion suggests the light path changed, and the samples read since the last check should be read again.

Limits and next steps

  • It does not measure turbidity. The 90° geometry suits fluorescence and does not track culture density; no choice of channels recovers it. Cell density has to be measured separately, with a spectrophotometer.
  • Low shares scatter more. Below about 20% sfGFP culture, tubes lie further from the line, by tens of percent, because they are the most sensitive to how they were mixed (see The low end). Mixing them by serial dilution should reduce this; it has not yet been tested.
  • A light-path check is still to be added. Reading a fixed reference, such as the same blank tube, at intervals, as described above, is not yet part of the firmware or the measuring routine.
  • Experiments are not directly comparable. The reader was moved between sessions, so absolute counts differ from one experiment to the next (an empty chamber read about 310 in the grid session and 160 to 190 in the linearity series). Each experiment is therefore referenced to its own tube with no sfGFP, measured in the same session.
  • No reference comparison yet. The reader has not been compared with a plate reader, and it has not read an AHL-induced culture.

Building one

  1. Build

    Generate the parts below for your board's thickness and cut the jointed parts from one 450 × 300 mm sheet of the same board. Paint every part matte black, the enclosure on the inside only; glue the chambers to the deck, and set the deck on its rails inside the enclosure.

  2. Wire

    The circuit Wiring

  3. Run

    Seat the UVette with its 2 mm path toward the LED, close the lid, and press the button: five readings of about 22 seconds each, the first discarded and the other four averaged. In each session, also read a tube with no sfGFP; results are its F5/F3 subtracted from each sample's.

    How a reading is made

The laser-cut parts

The whole set of parts is generated in the browser from the measured thickness of the plywood. The optical layout stays fixed: the cuvette pocket, the heights and positions of the light holes, and the inside of every chamber do not change with thickness, and thicker board makes the walls grow outward. Tabs are 8 mm wide; slots and notches are made 0.1 mm smaller on each side to take up the laser's kerf. The jointed parts all fit one 450 × 300 mm sheet and must be cut from one batch of board, because every joint is sized from a single measured thickness. The small parts without joints fill the gaps around them; those that do not fit go on a second sheet of any board.

Assembly tip: the deck and the enclosure panels have symmetric outlines, so each also fits when turned around, but their holes are not symmetric and the parts carry no orientation marks. Before gluing, check that every hole lines up with what it serves: the LED, the cuvette, the sensor and the wire holes.

Checked before publishing: across 2.80–3.20 mm every check passes and all jointed parts fit one sheet; at 3.25 mm the generator refuses to produce files. The six enclosure panels, assembled in 3D from the generated outlines, close with no gaps and no overlaps at zero fit offset for every thickness from 2.8 to 3.2 mm; the default 0.1 mm per side is there to take up the laser's kerf, as described above.

The circuit

Everything runs from the ESP32's 3.3 V supply. The AS7341 and the OLED share one I²C bus at different addresses (0x39 and 0x3C); both boards carry their own pull-up resistors. The LED is driven directly: GPIO25 to 220 Ω to the LED to ground, switched by the firmware. The button connects GPIO13 to ground and uses the ESP32's internal pull-up. None of the pins used (13, 21, 22, 25) is a strapping pin that affects boot.
